High concurrency: the saturation knee

Waves of 8/32/64/128 closed-loop clients, journals where errors are data, an operational knee definition and one defended operating point; a measured T4 run and an interference stretch with a frozen cap.

Public reading draft under editorial revision. Published slides and labs remain available; notebook execution and learner validation have separate statuses on the topic page.

This chapter is an authoring draft for Topic 06 and follows the approved saturation-knee arc. The notebook mock profile validates the analysis pipeline on a declared synthetic queue; only an owner-run GPU profile on the pinned service produces measured evidence. Every illustrative curve in this chapter says so on its face.

One service, many simultaneous clients

The support-ticket classifier from Topics 04–05 answers one request well. This chapter asks what happens when many arrive at once: where throughput stops growing, which resource runs out first, what errors and tail latency do at that boundary, and how to defend one operating point under a service-level objective. The course leaderboard already scores the geometric mean of output throughput at 32 and 64 concurrent clients — this chapter teaches you to read the curve you are being graded on.

Three habits carry the whole investigation. Errors are data: a timed-out request is a record with a terminal outcome, never a number to drop. The tail speaks before the mean: p95 crosses product deadlines while the average still looks healthy. And a prediction frozen before measurement beats a good story written after it.

Where request time goes

A request has several client-side clocks on one monotonic origin: logical arrival, admission to the client call, first content byte, terminal. First-token time (TTFT) and time per output token (TPOT) are defined on these clocks. Waiting before the first token is not service; a missing first-content timestamp is not zero seconds.

Why throughput grows before anything saturates

Every forward pass reads all model weights once, no matter how many requests share that pass. With FP16 weights of the pinned 3B model at about 6.2 GiB and 32 generated tokens per request, one lone request moves roughly 198 MiB of weights per generated token; sixty-four requests sharing their passes move about 3.1 MiB per token. Nothing got faster — the same read served more work.

Two finite resources

The rising curve ends at a ceiling made of two resources. Compute: prefill of a newly admitted request is a full extra pass that competes with every resident decode. KV memory: each retained position costs 2 (K and V) × 36 layers × 2 KV heads × 128 features × 2 bytes = 36 KiB (axes from the pinned configuration) — GQA is why this number is small; 16 query heads share 2 KV heads. A 2048-token context therefore bills about 72 MiB per request.

On a 15.36 GiB T4 with 6.2 GiB (6350 MiB) of weights, the arithmetic ceiling is (15360 − 6350) ÷ 72 ≈ 125 simultaneous full-context requests. That is an upper bound, not a scheduler promise: fragmentation, activation memory and admission policy each take a share. The lab freezes this prediction before measuring and returns to the gap afterward — the gap is the lesson, and each missing request has a name.

Continuous batching under load

Continuous batching runs one compute step at a time for all resident requests. The timeline below holds this chapter\'s central mechanism: when a new request is admitted, its prefill needs a whole step, and every resident request waits that step — TPOT jumps although nothing failed. When KV runs short, the scheduler preempts a request and later recomputes its early tokens: work is repeated, not free.

Read latency curves through this mechanism. Latency growing without errors is prefill interference. Completions stopping is admission control. Duplicated early tokens are preemption recompute. Same graph, three different mechanisms, three different fixes.

Queues and tails

A closed-loop client sends its next request only after the previous one terminates. WithCCC clients, mean residence RRR and think timeZZZ, offered demand is λ≈C/(R+Z)\lambda \approx C/(R+Z)λ≈C/(R+Z)— Little\'s law in closed form. A fixed client count therefore delivers falling demand as the service slows: the system throttles itself. This is why the lab defines waves by concurrency and reports offered and completed rates together; their divergence is the queue.

One experiment, everything else frozen

The lab runs waves of 8, 32, 64 and 128 concurrent closed-loop clients. Exactly one factor varies. Held fixed: the pinned model and revision, frozen public corpus texts, max tokens, temperature, seed, timeout budget, and a driver that never retries — a retry silently doubles demand and hides the failure it retried. Every terminated attempt writes one journal record with client-side monotonic timestamps and a terminal outcome: completed, timeout, server error, connection error or truncation.

An unaudited journal invalidates every number built on it. Four documented lies: missing terminals, duplicate IDs, foreign wave tags, impossible timestamps. The lab audits every wave before analysis and rejects damaged waves rather than repairing them.

Reading the curves

A saturation curve has three zones. Rise: batching amortizes weight traffic and tokens get cheaper per client. Flatten: compute and KV fill; extra clients mostly buy queue position. Collapse: wall budgets turn into timeouts and errors absorb the demand. Three ways these plots lie: dropped-error denominators, mean-only latency, silent retries — each has a required lab defense.

The first measured run tells a humbler story than the illustrative collapse. On a T4 with the frozen A-short corpus, output throughput rose 68 → 105 → 347 → 460 tok/s across the four waves with zero timeouts: natural-EOS classification replies carry about two output tokens each, and that workload never saturated this GPU inside the tested range. The knee lands at wave 64 by the gain rule — the last doubling that still paid ≥50%. Read the run's limits before quoting it: burst waves of three requests per client over one-to-two-second windows, a single run with visible jitter (p95 TTFT spiked to 1241 ms at wave 32, above the calmer 676 ms at 128), and nothing like the judge protocol's sustained ignore_eos load. A workload that does not saturate the box is a finding, not a failure of the experiment — the operational knee definition still answers, and the gap to the arithmetic ceiling of 125 remains a live question for heavier loads.

Stretch: a second application arrives

The document classifier from the interference study now shares the server: same weights, a different system prompt — which is not tenant isolation and not two replicas. Interference is measured honestly on each application separately, never averaged into one bar. The protection under test is an in-flight cap K ∈ {1, 2, 4, 8, 16} limiting the second application\'s concurrency. The protocol is the discipline: compare A-only against A+B at each K on a development pair, freeze one K with a written forecast, then run a distinct held-out pair once.

What this run does and does not establish

The notebook mock profile validates the entire analysis pipeline — journals, audits, curves, knee detection, freeze discipline — on a declared synthetic queue. Its numbers are not evidence about any real serving system. The run record for the measured figure is public (seminars/runs/2026-10-18-topic06-t4-saturation); the raw journal stays private. Accept, reject and inconclusive are all legitimate experimental verdicts; the freeze is what is graded.

Check understanding and transfer

Answer before opening the explanations. Questions 1, 6 and 8 change one condition: reproducing the notebook is not enough unless you can predict what changes next.

  1. Budget another configuration. A model has 40 layers, 8 KV heads, head width 80, FP16 cache. Compute the KV bytes per retained position and the ceiling on the same 15.36 GiB T4 with 6.35 GiB weights and 2048-token contexts.
  2. Weight traffic halves. Doubling concurrent clients from 8 to 16 doubles the average batch. What happens to weight bytes moved per generated token, and which cost does this NOT change?
  3. Latency without errors. During a wave, resident requests show TPOT jumping from 15 ms to 31 ms for exactly one step while outcomes stay completed. Name two mechanisms that fit, and one measurement to separate them.
  4. Fixed clients, falling demand. Eight closed-loop clients see mean residence rise from 0.25 s to 0.50 s with zero think time. What offered rate do they deliver before and after? Why does a fixed client count not fix demand?
  5. A flattering curve. A report shows throughput flat at 64 and 128 clients after excluding timed-out requests. What is hidden, and which two lab artifacts would expose it?
  6. Move the knee. On throughput 8→400, 32→720, 64→840, 128→580 tok/s, where is the knee with a 50 percent gain bar? With a 15 percent bar? What does the difference teach?
  7. Choose under an SLO. p95 TTFT: 8→0.35 s, 32→0.70 s, 64→1.10 s; error rates all zero. SLO: p95 at most 0.8 s. Which wave do you choose, what binds, and what would you check before trusting the choice?
  8. Defend a cap. At K = 1 application A is unharmed but B completes 12 of 48; at K = 16 B finishes everything and A's p95 TTFT triples. Your K = 4 run shows A's p95 up 20 percent and B at 46 of 48. Freeze or explore? What must precede the held-out pair?
Reveal eight answers and their limits
  1. Budget another configuration. Per position: 2 × 40 × 8 × 80 × 2 = 102,400 bytes = 100 KiB. Per request: 100 KiB × 2048 = 200 MiB. Ceiling: (15360 − 6350) MiB ÷ 200 MiB = 45 requests. Larger KV-head count raises per-request cost fivefold versus the pinned 2-KV-head configuration, so far fewer full contexts fit.
  2. Weight traffic halves. With 6.2 GiB weights and 32 tokens: 6.2 GiB / (8 × 32) = 24.8 MiB per token at 8 clients, 12.4 MiB at 16 — the same full weight read serves twice the requests per pass. It does not change per-request KV growth, activation memory, or the prompt-side prefill cost each new request pays. Cheaper tokens are not free capacity: a different resource still bounds the system.
  3. Latency without errors. A new request's prefill took the whole compute step (prefill interference), or a preemption forced a recompute pass. Both are scheduler events, not failures. Per-request journals with first-byte timestamps separate them: a prefill insertion coincides with a new admission; preemption leaves a visible restart in one request's token stream.
  4. Fixed clients, falling demand. 8 ÷ 0.25 = 32 req/s before; 8 ÷ 0.50 = 16 req/s after. In a closed loop each client waits for its previous reply, so a slowing service throttles its own demand. This is why the lab defines waves by concurrency and reports offered and completed rates together: their divergence is the queue.
  5. A flattering curve. Timeouts removed from the denominator hide collapse: at 128 the excluded requests are precisely the signal. The lab's outcome-share plot per wave (all terminated requests) and the per-wave audit summary expose it; throughput is computed over a wall window that includes failed requests' time.
  6. Move the knee. Gains: 8→32 is +80 percent, 32→64 is +16.7 percent, 64→128 is −31 percent. With a 50 percent bar no doubling qualifies, so the knee is the first wave, 8. With a 15 percent bar the 8→32 gain still clears it, so the knee is 32. The knee is an operational definition, not a physical constant; state the threshold with the claim.
  7. Choose under an SLO. Wave 32: the largest wave under the SLO; p95 TTFT binds because the error rate is clean everywhere. Before trusting it: audit the journal, confirm the denominator includes all attempts, check p50 alongside p95 for a bimodal split, and verify the run used the pinned model and frozen prompts.
  8. Defend a cap. Freeze K = 4 with a written forecast: both applications stay useful (A degraded, not broken; B nearly complete). What precedes held-out is the freeze itself — the policy file with K and the forecast, written before the held-out pair runs. Changing K after seeing held-out results converts validation into tuning.
