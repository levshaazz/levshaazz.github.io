High concurrency: the saturation knee

Waves of 8/32/64/128 closed-loop clients, journals where errors are data, an operational knee definition and one defended operating point; a measured T4 run and an interference stretch with a frozen cap.

One service, many simultaneous clients

The support-ticket classifier from Topics 04–05 answers one request well. This chapter asks what happens when many arrive at once: where throughput stops growing, which resource runs out first, what errors and tail latency do at that boundary, and how to defend one operating point under a service-level objective. The course leaderboard already scores the geometric mean of output throughput at 32 and 64 concurrent clients — this chapter teaches you to read the curve you are being graded on.

Begin with the incident. The service is healthy at 8 users: replies arrive in fractions of a second, every deadline met. At the rush hour, 128 simultaneous clients arrive, and three things happen at once: requests start dying at their wall-clock budgets (the client, not the server, gives up); users refresh their tabs, silently doubling the offered demand; and the replies that do arrive are late enough to miss product deadlines even though their content is correct. Nothing about the model changed — same weights, same GPU, same code. The question "what ran out?" has exactly two prime suspects, and the whole chapter is a disciplined investigation of which one, when, and what honest evidence looks like.

Three habits carry the whole investigation. Errors are data: a timed-out request is a record with a terminal outcome, never a number to drop. The tail speaks before the mean: p95 crosses product deadlines while the average still looks healthy. And a prediction frozen before measurement beats a good story written after it.

A note on scope, because the topic is deliberately narrow in one dimension: concurrency changes the time and memory behavior of the service, not the quality of the model's answers. We will not re-litigate classification accuracy here; Topics 03 and 04 own that question. What changes with load is whether an answer arrives at all, how late it is, and how many simultaneous askers the box can honestly sustain. Keeping those axes separate is what makes the investigation tractable — and it is also operationally realistic: in production, the person debugging saturation is rarely the person debugging prompt quality, and conflating the two dashboards wastes both teams' time. When you finish this chapter you should be able to say of any latency incident: "this is a queueing story, a scheduler story, or a model story" — and be right, with evidence.

Finally, a word to the grading contract. The course leaderboard scores the geometric mean of output throughput at exactly 32 and 64 concurrent clients, with a p95 TTFT gate at 64. Those two points sit deliberately in the region where saturation begins for this workload. Understanding why those numbers were chosen — and what a submission optimizes when it chases them — is not exam trivia; it is the difference between tuning blindly and tuning against a declared target. Everything in the next sections builds toward making that target legible.

Anatomy of the incident: three simultaneous failures

It is worth slowing down on what "the service broke" actually means, because three distinct failures hide inside that phrase, and each is measured differently. The first isstarvation: a request waits so long in queue that its wall-clock budget expires. The journal sees this as a timeout — a record that terminates without ever reaching first content. The second is silent demand amplification: a human user who times out refreshes the page, and the client sends a second, identical request. To the service this is new demand; to the product, the first request still failed. A driver that retries quietly makes every measurement lie — which is why the lab driver never retries, and why "no retries" is a rule you carry into any production load test. The third issoft degradation: answers keep arriving, but late — late enough to break a promise to a user even though the HTTP status is 200 and the label is correct. Throughput dashboards look fine under soft degradation; tail-latency dashboards do not. A useful saturation study reports all three: outcome shares, offered versus completed rates, and tail percentiles.

Notice what is not on this list: the model getting worse. Classification quality is upstream of this chapter. If the model mislabels tickets at one client, it mislabels them at 128 clients too. Concurrency changes time and memory, not semantics — and one of the disciplines of this topic is keeping those measurements separate.

What a request is made of

Before any queue forms, understand what each arriving request carries, because the prefill work of a request is proportional to its full rendered size — not just to the human text inside it. Every request to the classifier is a rendered prompt: a system instruction ("Choose ONE label for the current unresolved support issue…"), the user's ticket text, and the chat-template wrapper that binds them.

On the frozen teaching corpus, application A's tickets cluster around a hundred rendered tokens, while application B's documents run five times longer. The lab visualizes this anatomy directly: a stacked bar per application splits each rendered prompt into the system instruction and chat wrapper before the user text, the user text itself, and the wrapper tail after it. For A, nearly the whole bar is user text — the "payload" dominates the envelope. For B, the envelope takes visible share: a five-hundred-token document still carries its wrapper, and the scheduler processes every token of it during prefill. The numbers are in characters — an honest proxy — because exact tokenization belongs to the engine run; the proportions are what teach.

The anatomy matters for two reasons. First, prefill cost scales with the whole rendered prompt: B pays five times the prefill of A even when its "useful" text is what you care about. Second, the wrapper is not free noise — it is part of what the scheduler must process, and in the stretch section two applications with very different length profiles will share one server, competing for the same prefill steps. When you later read a waterfall chart and wonder why one request's service segment is longer than another's, the answer often starts here, in the anatomy of what arrived.

Where request time goes

A request has several client-side clocks on one monotonic origin: logical arrival, admission to the client call, first content byte, terminal. First-token time (TTFT) and time-per-output-token (TPOT) are defined on these clocks. Waiting before the first token is not service; a missing first-content timestamp is not zero seconds.

Run the diagnosis game on this decomposition. If TTFT doubles but TPOT holds, the request waited longer in queue — an admission or capacity problem upstream of compute. If TPOT doubles but TTFT holds, the decode steps themselves got slower — interference inside the batch, most often someone else's prefill stealing a step (the next section explains why). If both double, you are approaching a boundary where neither queue nor service can absorb the load. This reading is the single most transferable skill of the topic: latency numbers are not adjectives ("slow") but sentences with subjects (which stage) and verbs (grew).

Why throughput grows before anything saturates

Every forward pass reads all model weights once, no matter how many requests share that pass. With FP16 weights of the pinned 3B model at about 6.2 GiB and 32 generated tokens per request, one lone request moves roughly 198 MiB of weights per generated token; sixty-four requests sharing their passes move about 3.1 MiB per token. Nothing got faster — the same read served more work.

The deeper way to read the formula: batching converts idle memory bandwidth into throughput. A single request leaves the GPU's weight-reading capacity almost entirely wasted — you pay the full 6.2 GiB of traffic to produce one tiny classification. As concurrency rises, each weight byte fetched does proportionally more useful work, and throughput climbs. The curve cannot rise forever, though, because batching does not multiply the other finite resources: KV memory grows per request (not per batch), and every step of compute — including the prefill of each newly admitted request — still costs a full forward pass. The rising part of the saturation curve is amortization; the flattening part is everything amortization cannot touch.

Two finite resources

The rising curve ends at a ceiling made of two resources. Compute: prefill of a newly admitted request is a full extra pass that competes with every resident decode. KV memory: each retained position costs 2 (K and V) × 36 layers × 2 KV heads × 128 features × 2 bytes = 36 KiB (axes from the pinned configuration) — GQA is why this number is small; 16 query heads share 2 KV heads. A 2048-token context therefore bills about 72 MiB per request.

On a 15.36 GiB T4 with 6.2 GiB (6350 MiB) of weights, the arithmetic ceiling is (15360 − 6350) ÷ 72 ≈ 125 simultaneous full-context requests. That is an upper bound, not a scheduler promise: fragmentation, activation memory and admission policy each take a share. The lab freezes this prediction before measuring and returns to the gap afterward — the gap is the lesson, and each missing request has a name.

There is also a fundamentally different answer: KV compression. Multi-head Latent Attention (MLA), open-sourced by DeepSeek in their FlashMLA kernels, compresses K and V into a shared latent representation that reduces the per-position cost by roughly an order of magnitude. The arithmetic ceiling we computed (125 requests) assumes standard multi-head attention; an MLA-based model on the same GPU holds far more resident contexts because each one bills less. The knobs of Topic 07 move the ceiling; MLA removes it by changing what is stored. Both approaches answer the same observation: KV memory is the binding resource for long-context workloads. Practice turning the two-resource frame into predictions. A workload of long prompts and one-token answers (a search reranker) will hit the KV wall first: memory is billed by context, not by output. A workload of short prompts and long generations (a story writer) will hit compute first: decode steps dominate, and the KV bill accrues slowly. The ticket classifier sits closer to the first case — which is why the memory ceiling is the right back-of-envelope for this chapter, and why the measured T4 run (short prompts, natural-EOS answers of about two tokens) never approached it. When your workload changes shape, redo this paragraph from scratch; the arithmetic is cheap, and it is the difference between a tuned guess and a prediction.

Continuous batching under load

Continuous batching runs one compute step at a time for all resident requests. The timeline below holds this chapter's central mechanism: when a new request is admitted, its prefill needs a whole step, and every resident request waits that step — TPOT jumps although nothing failed. When KV runs short, the scheduler preempts a request and later recomputes its early tokens: work is repeated, not free.

Walk one hypothetical minute to make the mechanism concrete. Suppose four requests are residents, each earning one token per step, and a fifth arrives. Step 1: the scheduler must build the newcomer's KV — a full forward pass over its prompt — so all five requests are scheduled but only the newcomer actually advances (its prefill completes). Steps 2 through 60: all five decode together, and amortization resumes. The newcomer's arrival cost each resident exactly one token-step of delay — invisible in any per-request error count, visible as a one-step TPOT blip in the journal, and visible on the waterfall as a brief widening of every grey band at that moment. Now suppose KV memory runs short at some later step: the scheduler preempts the request with the least progress, discards its computed KV, and lets the freed memory serve the others. When the victim is re-admitted, its prompt is prefetched again — recomputed from scratch. The victim's latency doubles for a reason that never touches its own output quality. These two events — insertion stall and preemption recompute — are the entire "hidden" cost of mixing arriving work with ongoing work, and both are purely scheduler-level: no kernel is slower, no weight is different.

Read latency curves through this mechanism. Latency growing without errors is prefill interference. Completions stopping is admission control. Duplicated early tokens are preemption recompute. Same graph, three different mechanisms, three different fixes — and only the journal, with per-request timestamps, can tell you which one you are looking at. It is worth stressing the counterintuitive part: the scheduler is not "slow" when these events happen. It is making locally reasonable trade-offs under contention — finish the newly admitted prefill now so the request can start earning tokens; evict a victim so a promising request fits. Production engines push this further with an async scheduler: the CPU prepares the next batch while the GPU computes the current one, eliminating the dead time between steps. DeepInfer (DeepSeek's open-source engine, Apache-2.0, October 2026) made this the default; vLLM is moving the same direction. On the waterfall, the effect is visible as uniformly narrower grey bands — the scheduler is never idle waiting for the GPU to finish before deciding what runs next. Saturation is not a malfunction of the scheduler but the consequence of requests whose combined demand no longer fits the step budget. That is why the remedies of this topic are measurement and choice of operating point, not "fixing" the engine — engine knobs arrive in Topic 07.

Queues and tails

A closed-loop client sends its next request only after the previous one terminates. WithCCC clients, mean residence RRR and think timeZZZ, offered demand is λ≈C/(R+Z)\lambda \approx C/(R+Z)λ≈C/(R+Z)— Little's law in closed form. A fixed client count therefore delivers falling demand as the service slows: the system throttles itself. This is why the lab defines waves by concurrency and reports offered and completed rates together; their divergence is the queue.

The tail deserves its own paragraph, because it is where product reality lives. Under queueing near capacity, the distribution of wait times stretches: most requests still do fine (the median barely moves) while an unlucky minority waits many times longer than before. Percentiles like p95 exist precisely to make that minority visible. A product promise of "every ticket classified within a second" is a p100 promise — the strictest possible — and near saturation it is the first casualty. When you choose an operating point later in this chapter, you are really choosing how much tail you are willing to sell: every notch of extra throughput buys queue position, and the buyers of that queue position are your p95 requests. Notice also the asymmetry of trust: users forgive a consistently slow service more readily than an unpredictably fast one. A p50 of 400 ms with p95 of 900 ms feels worse in production than a steady 600 ms, even though the means are identical — one more reason the mean alone is not a service description.

Little's law also gives you a quick honesty check on any load test you did not run yourself. If a report claims 128 concurrent clients sustaining, say, 40 requests per second, then mean residence is at least 128 ÷ 40 = 3.2 seconds — and every latency number in that report must be consistent with at least that much residence for the population as a whole. When the claimed "average latency" is far below what the concurrency-to-throughput ratio implies, one of three things happened: clients were not actually closed-loop (they slept between requests), the latency was measured only over a surviving subset, or the numbers came from different runs. You now have the arithmetic to catch all three from a paragraph of marketing text — use it.

One more property of closed loops deserves words because students meet it as a surprise: a closed-loop system is self-stabilizing in a way an open-loop one is not. If the service slows down, offered demand falls, which relieves pressure, which lets the service speed back up — the loop dampens oscillation. An open-loop generator (fixed requests per second regardless of response) does the opposite: slowdown causes queue growth causes further slowdown, with no natural brake until budgets expire. Production traffic is usually a mixture: human users are closed loops with think time; cron jobs and batch workers are open loops. The lab uses the closed form because it makes concurrency the single honest dial, but when you interpret results, remember which kind of world you were modeling.

One experiment, everything else frozen

The lab runs waves of 8, 32, 64 and 128 concurrent closed-loop clients. Exactly one factor varies. Held fixed: the pinned model and revision, frozen public corpus texts, max tokens, temperature, seed, timeout budget, and a driver that never retries — a retry silently doubles demand and hides the failure it retried. Every terminated attempt writes one journal record with client-side monotonic timestamps and a terminal outcome: completed, timeout, server error, connection error or truncation.

The design principle generalizes beyond this lab: a curve is only about its varied factor if everything else is pinned. The moment two things drift between your measurement points — different prompts, a warmed-up cache here and a cold one there — the shape you draw is a composite of your drift, not of your factor. This is also why the wave order is fixed and why each wave's journal is kept whole: any later suspicion ("was wave 64 unusual?") can be answered from records, not from memory. And the audit habits belong here, before any analysis: a trusted journal has no missing terminals, no duplicate request IDs, no foreign wave tags, no impossible timestamps (terminal before arrival). The lab'saudit_records classifies such damage and never repairs it — an unaudited number is an unowned number.

The figure above is the chapter's memento about denominators: three completed replies and two correct labels among six arrivals give both 2/3 (conditional accuracy) and 1/6 (useful arrivals), and both are honest answers to different questions. Choosing which question you are answering — before looking at the data — is most of the intellectual work of reporting.

Reading the request waterfall

The lab's most direct picture is the waterfall: one horizontal bar per request, drawn from the journal you just produced. Each bar starts when the request arrived, shows a light-grey segment while it waited for its first token, a dark-blue segment while it was served, and — if the request never made it — a red bar ending at the moment the client's wall budget expired. A vertical dotted line marks that budget; bars that touch it are the timeouts.

Read a waterfall in three passes. First the population: are you looking at all requests of the wave or a sample, and does the title say which? (The lab samples evenly across arrival time so both survivors and casualties stay visible — a sample of "the first forty arrivals" would show mostly the lucky ones.) Second the texture: on a small wave, grey segments are thin and blue work dominates — waiting is not yet a cost. As concurrency grows, grey creeps rightward: the queue is eating the budget before service even begins. Third the red: bars that die before any blue at all are requests that never started being served — starvation in the plain sense. The waterfall is also your best cross-check on the clock decomposition from earlier: TTFT is exactly the light-grey width, and a TPOT jump that looked mysterious in a table becomes, on the waterfall, a visible step where someone else's prefill occupied the machine.

One warning about the medium: a waterfall is a sample of individual stories, not an aggregate. It convinces; it does not summarize. Use it to explain a mechanism you already measured with curves, never as a substitute for the outcome shares and percentiles that count every request.

Reading the curves

A saturation curve has three zones. Rise: batching amortizes weight traffic and tokens get cheaper per client. Flatten: compute and KV fill; extra clients mostly buy queue position. Collapse: wall budgets turn into timeouts and errors absorb the demand. Three ways these plots lie: dropped-error denominators, mean-only latency, silent retries — each has a required lab defense.

Learn to narrate a specific curve, not just the archetype. For each wave, the reading order is: outcome shares first (what fraction even completed?), then throughput (at what price in tokens per second?), then p50 and p95 together (who waited, and how unevenly?). A curve that flattens with clean outcomes is a capacity boundary politely reached. A curve that stays high while errors climb is collapse wearing a disguise — the graph of survivors. The lab makes the disguise impossible by construction: failed requests keep their wall-clock share of the denominator, so collapsing waves lose throughput even when the survivors, measured alone, would look excellent.

The gain rule rewards a habit worth naming: never report a "knee" without its γ. Two teams comparing knees measured under different thresholds are comparing nothing. The same applies to percentile choice (p95 versus p99), timeout budget, and wave spacing — every operational definition is part of the result, and the memo template in the lab forces you to state the ones that matter.

What the measured run said

Theory gives the shape; only a measured run gives the position. The published T4 run of 21 October 2026 (owner-executed on the current notebook, single run, burst waves of three requests per client, natural-EOS replies of about two tokens — read the limits before quoting) produced the numbers this course now uses as its measured anchor.

Throughput rose 29 → 208 → 306 → 311 tok/s across the four waves with zero timeouts at every wave, and then flattened: the last doubling (64 → 128 clients) bought +2%. The knee lands at wave 32 by the gain rule — and this matched the frozen arithmetic prediction exactly, the rare benchmarking day when the sentence written before the run survives contact with the measurement. Two details earn their own sentences. First, wave 8 is warmup data: its wall time (1.66 s, the longest of all four waves) and its p95 TTFT (1270 ms) are first-wave CUDA-graph capture, not steady-state behaviour — a cold-start wave inside your measurement is a confound to name, not an outlier to drop. Second, after warmup the tail tells the real story: p95 TTFT went 324 → 478 → 1076 ms while throughput went flat. Throughput alone said "nothing changed after 64"; the tail said the box was filling up. Any throughput curve that plateaus hides exactly this — which is why the lab plots outcomes, throughput and percentiles as three separate pictures before it lets you speak. The honest postscript: this workload now has three runs, and the knee came out 64 → 32 → 64 across them (18 October, 21 October anchor, 21 October replicate). The reason is not mystery noise: the 32 → 64 doubling gained 47 percent in one run and 56 percent in another — the gain sits exactly at the fifty-percent bar of the gain rule, so single-run jitter flips the verdict by one wave. All three runs agree on what matters (zero timeouts, no collapse inside the tested range, the tail rising into wave 128). The knee itself is an operational definition sitting on a threshold — quote the threshold, the bar and the run count with every knee claim, and treat a knee that flips between adjacent waves under replication as a finding about the workload, not a failure of the method.

The general lesson compounds with the two-resource frame: predict first (which resource, given the shape of this workload?), then measure, then explain any gap in the direction the anatomy points — long-prompt workloads die by memory, long-generation workloads by compute, and short-everything workloads may not die inside your tested range, only plateau while the tail grows. The corollary for practice: before you benchmark anything, write the sentence "this workload is long in [prompts/outputs/neither]" and compute the corresponding budget — it takes one minute and it predicts which curve shape you are about to draw. Teams that skip the sentence end up benchmarking the wrong resource and tuning knobs that cannot help.

Choose an operating point under an SLO

The leaderboard is exactly this question. The judge scoresS=tok/s32⋅tok/s64S=\sqrt{\mathrm{tok/s}_{32}\cdot\mathrm{tok/s}_{64}}S=tok/s32​⋅tok/s64​​ with a p95 TTFT gate at 64 clients; the current round bars live on the leaderboard page, not in this chapter, so they cannot drift out of date here. The lab rule is the same shape: the largest wave whose p95 TTFT and error rate both clear the SLO, with the binding constraint named in writing, compared against your frozen prediction, plus one alternative explanation the run cannot exclude.

Three honest verdicts exist. Accept: the chosen point meets the SLO on evidence you would show a stranger. Reject: no point qualifies — the product needs a smaller promise or a bigger machine, and saying so is the job. Inconclusive: the run cannot distinguish "healthy" from "lucky" — a single burst wave with clean outcomes and near-SLO tails is exactly this, and the professional answer is another run, not a hopeful sentence in the memo. The lab grades the freeze, not the fortune: a falsifiable choice with a written forecast is full marks even when the held-out answer disappoints.

It helps to see the memo as a contract with three named parties. The productowner reads the SLO line and the chosen wave: this is what we promise users, at this load. The capacity planner reads the knee and the ceiling: this is where the curve bends and why, in the language of the two resources. The skeptic — every good memo is written for one — reads the alternative-explanation line: the single most plausible story under which this choice is wrong, stated before anyone can accuse the author of cherry-picking. A memo that cannot name its skeptic's best argument is not a decision; it is a hope with formatting. The three-sentence template in the lab notebook is deliberately minimal — chosen wave versus prediction, binding resource with evidence, unexcluded alternative — because every real postmortem you will ever write contains exactly those three parts, buried under more prose.

And a final connection to the round you are actually graded on: the judge's score is the geometric mean at 32 and 64 clients precisely so that neither point can be sacrificed for the other. A submission tuned to look brilliant at 32 and collapse at 64 scores poorly; so does the reverse. Geometric means punish imbalance — which is, in miniature, the whole lesson of choosing operating points under an SLO rather than chasing a single maximum.

Stretch: a second application arrives

The document classifier from the interference study now shares the server: same weights, a different system prompt — which is not tenant isolation and not two replicas. Interference is measured honestly on each application separately, never averaged into one bar. The protection under test is an in-flight cap K ∈ {1, 2, 4, 8, 16} limiting the second application's concurrency. The protocol is the discipline: compare A-only against A+B at each K on a development pair, freeze one K with a written forecast, then run a distinct held-out pair once.

Why a cap and not a faster queue? Because interference here has a specific mechanism: B's long prefills occupy scheduler steps that A's decodes were using. Limiting how many B requests may be in flight simultaneously bounds how many steps per unit time B can claim — a blunt, predictable, cheap instrument. The trade is equally direct: smaller K protects A's tails but starves B's throughput (K = 0 is forbidden — refusing all B work fails the second product). The dev table shows the frontier; the frozen K picks a point on it; held-out asks whether the point generalizes to texts the choice never saw. Every idea here — separate measurements, predeclared choice, held-out validation — is the Topic 03 calibration discipline returning at the systems level, which is the quiet thesis of the whole course: the experimental method scales from tensors to services.

A practical detail that decides whether the stretch teaches or misleads: the dev pair and the held-out pair must differ in texts, not in protocol. Same waves, same cap candidates, same journal fields — new documents and new tickets. If the protocol also changes (different wave size, different budget), a failed held-out tells you nothing about the freeze: it might be the texts, it might be the new conditions, and you have spent your one honest validation run to learn neither. This is the same discipline as Topic 03's calibration-versus-test split, and it fails the same way in the wild: teams "validate" against a run whose load shape quietly differs from development, then ship a policy that was never tested under its real conditions.

Read the dev table as a frontier, not a ranking. Each row (one K) buys B throughput at an A-latency price; there is no row that is best at both, and the interesting rows are where the exchange rate changes steeply. A cap of 1 buys A near-perfect tails at the cost of B crawling; 16 lets B sprint while A's p95 triples. The knee logic from the main lab reappears here in a second costume: choose the last cheap step before the price spikes, write down what you expect, and let held-out arbitrate. Two cost curves, one method — which is, again, the quiet thesis.

Check understanding and transfer

Answer before opening the explanations. Questions 1, 6 and 8 change one condition: reproducing the notebook is not enough unless you can predict what changes next.

  1. Budget another configuration. A model has 40 layers, 8 KV heads, head width 80, FP16 cache. Compute the KV bytes per retained position and the ceiling on the same 15.36 GiB T4 with 6.35 GiB weights and 2048-token contexts.
  2. Weight traffic halves. Doubling concurrent clients from 8 to 16 doubles the average batch. What happens to weight bytes moved per generated token, and which cost does this NOT change?
  3. Latency without errors. During a wave, resident requests show TPOT jumping from 15 ms to 31 ms for exactly one step while outcomes stay completed. Name two mechanisms that fit, and one measurement to separate them.
  4. Fixed clients, falling demand. Eight closed-loop clients see mean residence rise from 0.25 s to 0.50 s with zero think time. What offered rate do they deliver before and after? Why does a fixed client count not fix demand?
  5. A flattering curve. A report shows throughput flat at 64 and 128 clients after excluding timed-out requests. What is hidden, and which two lab artifacts would expose it?
  6. Move the knee. On throughput 8→400, 32→720, 64→840, 128→580 tok/s, where is the knee with a 50 percent gain bar? With a 15 percent bar? What does the difference teach?
  7. Choose under an SLO. p95 TTFT: 8→0.35 s, 32→0.70 s, 64→1.10 s; error rates all zero. SLO: p95 at most 0.8 s. Which wave do you choose, what binds, and what would you check before trusting the choice?
  8. Defend a cap. At K = 1 application A is unharmed but B completes 12 of 48; at K = 16 B finishes everything and A's p95 TTFT triples. Your K = 4 run shows A's p95 up 20 percent and B at 46 of 48. Freeze or explore? What must precede the held-out pair?
Reveal eight answers and their limits
  1. Budget another configuration. Per position: 2 × 40 × 8 × 80 × 2 = 102,400 bytes = 100 KiB. Per request: 100 KiB × 2048 = 200 MiB. Ceiling: (15360 − 6350) MiB ÷ 200 MiB = 45 requests. Larger KV-head count raises per-request cost fivefold versus the pinned 2-KV-head configuration, so far fewer full contexts fit.
  2. Weight traffic halves. Weight bytes per token halve: the same full weight read serves twice the requests per pass. It does not change per-request KV growth, activation memory, or the prompt-side prefill cost each new request pays. Cheaper tokens are not free capacity: a different resource still bounds the system.
  3. Latency without errors. A new request's prefill took the whole compute step (prefill interference), or a preemption forced a recompute pass. Both are scheduler events, not failures. Per-request journals with first-byte timestamps separate them: a prefill insertion coincides with a new admission; preemption leaves a visible restart in one request's token stream.
  4. Fixed clients, falling demand. 8 ÷ 0.25 = 32 req/s before; 8 ÷ 0.50 = 16 req/s after. In a closed loop each client waits for its previous reply, so a slowing service throttles its own demand. This is why the lab defines waves by concurrency and reports offered and completed rates together: their divergence is the queue.
  5. A flattering curve. Timeouts removed from the denominator hide collapse: at 128 the excluded requests are precisely the signal. The lab's outcome-share plot per wave (all terminated requests) and the per-wave audit summary expose it; throughput is computed over a wall window that includes failed requests' time.
  6. Move the knee. Gains: 8→32 is +80 percent, 32→64 is +16.7 percent, 64→128 is −31 percent. With a 50 percent bar no doubling qualifies, so the knee is the first wave, 8. With a 15 percent bar the 8→32 gain still clears it, so the knee is 32. The knee is an operational definition, not a physical constant; state the threshold with the claim.
  7. Choose under an SLO. Wave 32: the largest wave under the SLO; p95 TTFT binds because the error rate is clean everywhere. Before trusting it: audit the journal, confirm the denominator includes all attempts, check p50 alongside p95 for a bimodal split, and verify the run used the pinned model and frozen prompts.
  8. Defend a cap. Freeze K = 4 with a written forecast: both applications stay useful (A degraded, not broken; B nearly complete). What precedes held-out is the freeze itself — the policy file with K and the forecast, written before the held-out pair runs. Changing K after seeing held-out results converts validation into tuning.
