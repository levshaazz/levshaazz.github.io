Adapters & multi-tenancy: LoRA economics

One frozen base serves many tenants through 0.22 MB LoRA adapters. The rank arithmetic prices the sliver; a declared 2 ms swap cost makes routing a two-sided ledger (switches against waits); affinity windows bound the trade; per-tenant p95 applies the T13 discipline at tenant grain. AdapterSim reproduces every dial row; the star challenge tunes the window under declared lines.

Editorially accepted material, reviewed by independent agents and checked for source agreement and rendering. This is not a human expert review or a completed learner pilot. Notebook execution and learner validation are separate statuses on the topic page.

Topic hubOpen slidesTry the PlaygroundPrimary sources

This chapter follows the approved Topic14 arc. The carrier is a DECLARED TEACHING MODEL OF A MULTI-TENANT SERVING LAYER: no real LoRA training happened (adapter quality deltas are declared), the 2 ms swap cost is a declared knob, and the tenant stream is the deck's six requests. The base size (491 MB) is the real T09 course engine; the journal canonical form is the real layer-2 gateway's (runtime/gateway/app.py). S-LoRA and Punica are cited as the real-systems line. Nothing here is a benchmark of any production multi-tenant stack.

A second team wants "its own model"

The demand arrives as a compliment: your inference service is good, and now a second team wants a version tuned to its own prompts — then a third, then a product line. The naive answer is honest arithmetic in the wrong direction: a copy of the engine per tenant. On the course engine that is 491 MB per copy; by the fifth team you operate 2.5 GB of weights that drifted five ways, load five times, and disagree at the worst moment. The multiplication problem scales every bad property with the tenant count, and none of the good ones.

LoRA — low-rank adaptation — collapses the multiplication. The base stays frozen; each tenant gets a small adapter, a pair of low-rank matrices per target projection, trained on the tenant's own prompts. Tenants share the base and differ by slivers. The chapter prices those slivers honestly: the rank arithmetic that sizes them, the switch that serving them costs, the routing policy that trades switches against waits, and the per-tenant measurement that keeps every promise falsifiable. Everything executable lives in lab 14 — the deck names the ideas, the lab makes them runnable in the browser, and the journal you already trust carries the tenant field that makes fairness auditable.

The adapter ledger

Freeze the ledger before any arithmetic — the discipline every topic here runs on. Five rows: the frozen base at 491 MB (the real T09 engine, shared by every tenant); the hidden size d = 896 (declared); the rank r = 16 (the quality/size dial); the four attention projections q, k, v, o as LoRA targets (the standard choice, declared); and the adapter's own size, which is not a row at all but the next section's computation. The ledger's point is provenance: every number is either the real course engine or a declared knob with a name.

The ledger also fixes what is NOT for sale. The adapter cannot change the tokenizer, the context window, the cutoff knowledge or the T02 memory physics — those belong to the frozen base and are shared by every tenant. When a tenant asks the adapter for a longer context, they are asking the wrong layer; the honest answer is that the base contract is shared, and only the tenant delta is purchasable. Posttest p1 asks exactly this distinction, because it is the one multi-tenancy claim most often overstated.

Adapter size: one multiplication

A LoRA update represents the weight change of a matrix as a product of two small matrices: A of shape r × d_in and B of shape d_out × r. Storing the update means storing r × (d_in + d_out) numbers per target matrix — the r is the rank, the dial that trades expressiveness for size. With four target matrices, fp16 storage, d = 896 and r = 16: 2 × 16 × 1792 × 4 = 114 688 parameters → 229 376 bytes ≈ 0.22 MB.

Two classic errors live in this formula and quiz q1 hunts both. Doubling the factor 2 twice — the "2" is fp16 bytes per parameter, not the A/B pair; the pair is already inside r × (d_in + d_out). Or dropping A/B entirely and counting only r × d — which understates the adapter by half. The exercise makes you recompute at rank 8 and rank 32, because the rank dial is the topic's version of the T10 degree: its honest setting comes from a frozen plan and a measured sweep, never from a leaderboard vibe.

Read the figure as the multiplication collapsing: the 491 MB base bar dwarfs the 0.22 MB adapter slivers by three orders of magnitude. That ratio — 0.05 percent of the base per tenant — is what makes multi-tenancy affordable at all, and it is why the interesting costs of this topic are not bytes but switches.

The switch: one loaded adapter at a time

At serving time one adapter is resident per engine slot. Requests for tenant A run with A's weights in place; the first request for tenant B pays an adapter load. The declared teaching model prices that load at 2 ms — flat, per switch, regardless of tenant. Real systems are smarter: S-LoRA pages adapter memory so adapters swap in chunks, and Punica merges adapter compute into custom kernels so the cost shrinks further. Both are cited as the real-systems line, and both are omitted here on purpose: a flat declared cost keeps the routing arithmetic hand-checkable, which is what makes it learnable.

The consequence that turns routing into economics is order-dependence. The total switch bill depends not on how many requests each tenant sent but on how their arrivals interleaved. Six requests A, B, A, B, A, B cost five switches in arrival order and one switch grouped into A, A, A, B, B, B. Same load, fivefold different bill — which means the switch count is a policy output, not a load fact. Exercise 2 makes you count it on any stream; quiz q2 counts it on the deck's worst case.

The switching drill

Walk the drill once slowly, because the capstone automates exactly it. Stream: A, B, A, B, A, B. FIFO: every adjacent pair differs → five swaps → 10 ms of pure switch time on six requests. Grouped: A, A, A, B, B, B → one swap → 2 ms. The fivefold difference is the switch tax collapsing under grouping.

Then the honest sentence, which the ribbon of the lab figure carries: the four missing swaps did not vanish — they became waiting. The second and third A waited for the first; likewise the Bs. Grouping is not free serving; it is a trade of switching for waiting, and a policy that quotes one side of that trade without the other is lying by omission. The capstone ledger prices both sides so neither can hide.

Three policies and one exchange rate

FIFO — serve in arrival order. Honest to arrivals, brutal to switches: the full tax, every tenant change priced at the declared 2 ms. The affinity window — fill a window of W arrivals, group inside it by tenant: the switch count collapses toward the tenant count per window, and the price is the wait a request pays for its group. Tenant-pinned queues — one queue per tenant, drained at will: minimal switches, real head-of-line waiting for small tenants stuck behind large ones.

One exchange rate prices all three: a swap is 2 ms of engine time. The policies trade that rate against waiting, and the waiting is measured per tenant — which is the next section's discipline. There is no free policy here; the exam question is not "which policy wins" but "what does each pay with, and can you see it in the journal".

The window is the dial

The affinity window W is the tuning knob between the degenerate policies. W = 1 is FIFO: every request its own batch, all switching. W = the stream length is tenant-pinned: one batch, all waiting. Between them, W bounds the wait a request can pay (at most W − 1 arrivals ahead of it in its own group) and bounds the tenant groups per window, which bounds the switches. On the declared six-request stream: W = 1 gives five swaps and zero waited; W = 3 gives two swaps and two waited; W = 6 gives one swap and four waited. The star challenge walks this dial with declared lines — swaps ≤ 2, waited ≤ 4, W ≤ 4 — and only one W passes all three, which is what "checkable policy" means.

Tenant p95: the T13 discipline, one grain finer

The T13 formula — nearest-rank p95 over a named window — applied globally is the mean-vs-tail mistake again, one abstraction level up. A global p95 over two tenants reports the fast tenant's health and hides the slow tenant behind it. The honest number slices the journal by tenant first: the tenant's own entries, sorted, element ceil(0.95 × n), a measured request — then the window named on top.

The small-sample caveat is honest and load-bearing: a tenant slice of 20 entries makes the p95 sensitive to a single request; short windows are noisy windows. The promise names the window anyway, because "p95 per tenant per week" is falsifiable while "our tenants are happy" is not. Lab exercise 4 computes the slice from the journal fixture; the fixture is the T13 journal shape with a tenant field added — the same hash chain, one grain finer.

The verdict picture: under mixed batching tenant B sits at p95 1.6 s against the declared 1.0 s SLO — B's slice inherits A's swaps and A's slow requests inside the shared batch. Affinity grouping pulls both tenants under the SLO at the price of batch waits. Routing is not a cost-only decision; it moves per-tenant promises, and the per-tenant slice is the instrument that makes the movement visible.

Quality per tenant: measured, not assumed

"Our adapter is better on our prompts" is a measurement claim and must survive the T13 invariant protocol: the tenant's frozen prompt slice, fixed order, seeded decoding, cache-busting, isolation — then a delta against the frozen base. A delta you cannot reproduce is weather. The delta is also per tenant by construction: the support adapter's gain lives on support prompts; quoting it as a global improvement is the same averaging lie in a new costume.

The rank dial's honest setting follows the same pattern as the T10 degree: a frozen plan, a sweep over ranks, a measured quality-per-tenant slice, a ledger row for the bytes. Rank 8 may keep most of the delta at half the bytes; rank 32 may buy two points and double the swap bytes. Neither claim is true in general — each is true or false on a measured slice, and the runbook row names the slice.

429 per tenant: fairness is auditable or it is fiction

The T08 admission envelope goes one grain finer: per tenant. A noisy tenant's burst must not consume a quiet tenant's seats, and the 429 ledger must be able to prove it. Every rejection entry carries the tenant field, the rule that fired, the timestamp and the Retry-After granted — then "who was rejected, by which rule, how often" is a query, not an argument. Without the tenant field the fairness question is unanswerable; with it, the audit is part of the runbook.

The T13 cost lens applies per tenant too: a tenant's cost per 1M output tokens is computed on its own measured slice — the large tenant's batching gain must not be quoted as the small tenant's bill. Weighted fair admission, where weights are declared and the weights' effect is visible in the ledger, is one honest policy among several; the auditable part is the journal, never the promise.

Two failure stories, one instrument

Story one: the smothered tenant. Mixed batching looks efficient globally; the global p95 is healthy; but tenant B's own slice sits at 1.6 s against a 1.0 s promise for a whole afternoon. Detection: the per-tenant slice, window named. Mitigation: affinity grouping, verified by the same slice. The postmortem cites B's entries, not the global chart that hid them.

Story two: the unmeasured upgrade. Every tenant silently upgraded to rank 32; the adapters doubled; a small tenant's slot started swapping twice as often and its p95 doubled. Detection: the swap-count ledger per window — the count is a policy output, remember. Mitigation: rank back to the measured per-tenant setting; the quality claim re-run under the invariant protocol per tenant. Both stories end at the same instrument: the journal with the tenant field.

What this model omits

Declared simplifications, in the open: no real LoRA training happened — adapter quality deltas are declared teaching numbers; no paged adapter memory (S-LoRA's answer); no merged kernels (Punica's answer); one resident adapter per slot; switches priced flat at 2 ms; the tenant stream is six requests plus the challenge's window walk. A production stack pages adapters, overlaps adapter compute, and prices switches by bytes moved — the constants move; the ledger, the drills and the capstone arithmetic stay.

Why it still holds: the method — price the sliver, count the order-dependent cost, measure promises per tenant, audit fairness from the journal — is independent of how the constant was derived. Deeper systems multiply the knobs, not the discipline of naming them. Say what the model omits before someone else discovers it.

The deliverable: the routing memo

The milestone artifact is one page someone else could execute: the ledger rows (rank, d, targets, adapter size at fp16); the policy you chose — FIFO, window W, or pinned queues — with its two-sided price on your own stream (swaps and waits, both counted); the per-tenant p95 table with windows named; and the fairness row — who was rejected by which rule. Checked against AdapterSim, the lab's capstone, so the memo's numbers are reproducible arithmetic, not prose.

These four fields are the T16 defense story in miniature: a contract (the ledger), a policy with its price, a measurement (the per-tenant table), and an audit (the fairness row). The capstone week will ask you to defend exactly this shape at larger scale — and the supervised agent will be aimed at exactly this surface.

Worked by hand: one routing decision end to end

Before trusting any simulator, do one decision with your hands — the discipline every topic here runs on. The stream is the deck's six requests; the policy under test is the affinity window W = 3; the slot starts holding adapter A; a swap costs the declared 2 ms. Window one covers arrivals 1–3: A, B, A. First-appearance order: A, then B. Group A = arrivals 1 and 3 (two requests); group B = arrival 2. Serve group A first — the slot already holds A, no swap; two requests waited behind the first A. Then group B — one swap, 2 ms; its single request waited for nothing beyond the group switch.

Window two covers arrivals 4–6: B, A, B. Groups: B first (arrival 4), then A (arrivals 5–6). The slot holds B from window one — group B is free; its request waited for nothing. Group A: one swap. Waiting: two requests in group A, plus arrival 5 and 6 behind arrival 4... careful — arrival 4 is B (first of its group), arrivals 5–6 are A (two waited). Window totals: one swap, two waited. Grand total: two swaps, four waited — which is exactly the dial-table row for W = 3. The simulator must reproduce this by construction; if it disagrees with your hands, either your grouping or your carry-across rule is wrong, and the hands win.

The exchange rate in practice

The declared 2 ms is the chapter's fixed exchange rate between switches and waiting, and real deployments have their own. Reading it honestly means asking three questions of any number you are handed. Is it flat — one price per switch regardless of tenants — or does it scale with adapter bytes (a rank-32 swap moves twice the bytes of a rank-16 one)? Is it measured — from the serving stack's own logs, with the measurement window named — or declared? And does the price hide overlaps — S-LoRA-style paging and Punica-style merged kernels make parts of the swap run alongside compute, which is a lower effective price, not a free one.

The lab's ledger keeps the rate flat and declared so the routing arithmetic is learnable; the runbook row for your own deployment replaces the constant with your measured one, window named. The exercises do not change: count swaps, group, price both sides. Only the units on the exchange line move — which is why they were named separately from the start.

Capacity planning with adapters

The ledger extends capacity planning by one column: per-tenant adapter bytes. The slot budget is the base plus the resident adapter plus headroom; because adapters are slivers, the resident set is nearly invariant to the tenant count — the planning question shifts from "how many copies fit" to "how many tenants share this slot before switching traffic dominates". That question has a journal answer: switches per window grow with tenant interleaving, so the slot's tenant budget is a routing property, not a memory property. A slot serving two heavy tenants alternately may pay more switch time than a slot serving five calm ones.

The honest planning artifact is therefore per slot and per week: the ledger row, the observed swap count over a named window, the wait per tenant, and the rank actually deployed. One table per slot replaces the instinctive "how many models can we pack" with the measured question the topic teaches: what did each tenant's presence cost this slot, in switches and in waits, and is the routing policy still the cheapest answer.

The tenant journal: retention and the fairness window

Fairness claims age. A rejection audit from six weeks ago proves what the stack did then, not what it does now — so the tenant journal's retention is a policy with a name. The course stack keeps everything (the journal is the grading surface); a production stack seals archives, publishes head hashes and thins live entries. Either way the fairness window must be named wherever the fairness claim is made: "rejections per tenant over the audited window" is a number; "we are fair" is a mood.

Cardinality returns at tenant grain too: the tenant field multiplies every aggregate by the tenant count. The slice-and-p95 discipline from T13 survives it — slice, sort, nearest-rank, window named — but the dashboards must compute the slices per tenant on purpose, not inherit a global aggregate and hope. Retention, cardinality and the named fairness window are the three policy lines the deliverable's runbook carries forward from T13.

The on-call day for adapters

Assemble the chapter as the shift it describes. Morning: the routing memo's policy is live — affinity window W = 3 on the busiest slot; the swap ledger reads two per window, the per-tenant p95 table holds both tenants under the SLO. Noon: a third tenant onboards with a rank-16 adapter; the ledger gains its sliver, the window groups it, the switches tick up one per window but hold under the line. Mid-afternoon: the noisy tenant bursts; its own queue absorbs the burst inside the window, the other tenants' slices stay under their p95s, and the 429 ledger shows the burst tenant's own rejections — fairness, visible from entries. Evening: a rank experiment for the new tenant — the frozen plan from T06 applied to the rank dial, measured on the tenant slice, ledger row updated with the bytes.

Now replay the day without the chapter's instruments. No tenant field: the burst is invisible in the global p95, the fairness question unanswerable. No window dial: the swaps are whatever FIFO pays, fivefold on alternating streams. No per-tenant slice: the new tenant's quality claim is a slogan. No ledger row: the rank experiment is a vibe with bytes attached. The day got harder only because the facts got expensive — and the instruments exist to keep them cheap.

Reading a multi-tenant dashboard critically

The inherited multi-tenant dashboard fails an audit in four familiar places. The global p95 tile — wrong grain: promises are per tenant; the tile hides B behind A. The swap-rate tile without a window — a number that names no policy output; the swap count is a property of the routing policy over a named window, and without the window it cannot be compared across weeks. The adapter-quality banner — a claim with no slice and no protocol; quality deltas live on tenant prompt slices under the invariant protocol or nowhere. The fairness panel without the ledger link — a badge where a query should be.

The honest rebuild is small and familiar from T13: one row per tenant per SLO with the slice named; one row per routing policy with its two-sided price over a named window; one cost ledger per tenant on measured slices; one link to the chain verifier. Four tiles with answers, beating forty tiles of inherited green — the T13 audit, applied one grain finer.

What the adapter does not fix: the prefix

Multi-tenancy tempts one more claim — "each tenant also gets its own prompt cache". The chapter refuses it, honestly: the adapter changes weights, not the KV cache layout, and a tenant's cached prefixes live in the shared cache under T05's block table. Two consequences follow. First, tenant isolation of cached state is a policy question (whose tokens may share a block), not an adapter property — the adapter neither isolates nor contaminates the cache. Second, the cache hit rate a tenant sees depends on its own prompt reuse and on eviction pressure from other tenants' prefixes: a busy new tenant can lower a quiet tenant's hit rate without either adapter changing. That coupling is measurable — hit rate per tenant, sliced from the journal like the p95 — and it belongs in the routing memo's table, because a policy that moves tenants between slots moves their cache neighborhoods too.

The exam voice is a boundary statement: adapters multiply tenants over weights; the cache multiplies nothing and forgets under pressure. A tenant promising "my adapter keeps my prefixes warm" has promised the wrong instrument — the honest promise names the block table, the eviction policy and the measured hit rate per tenant, window named. This is the same refusal as the context-width claim: the adapter is a weight delta, and weight deltas do not reach the cache's ledger.

Adapter versions are releases

The moment a tenant retrains its adapter, the serving layer hosts a new artifact with the old tenant's name — and T09's lesson applies verbatim: a floating tag is a deferred dependency break. The honest fleet versions adapters like images: a digest or content hash on the adapter file, a release note per version naming what the training slice was, and a rollback path that restores the previous version's bytes, not just its name. The routing memo's ledger row then cites the adapter digest, and the fairness audit can prove which version served the window it audits.

Version drift is the quiet failure the collapse was supposed to prevent: ten tenants with ten unversioned adapters drift as surely as ten unversioned engines — more quietly, because the base they share really is identical and the drift hides in 0.22 MB slivers. The lab's declared deltas are labeled by tenant and by rank for exactly this reason: a claim without a version is unauditable. The runbook row: "adapter B v7 → v8, digest changed, quality slice re-run under the invariant protocol, swap bytes +0.0 — the ledger row is the release note."

Comparing tenants without ranking them

Once promises are per tenant, someone will ask for a leaderboard of tenants — and the chapter refuses the leaderboard while keeping the comparison. The journal supports honest questions of the form "did tenant B's p95 improve after the routing change", because both sides of that sentence are measured slices with named windows. It does not support "which tenant is best", because tenants differ in domain, traffic shape and rank settings — a ranking would compare incomparable contracts and reward traffic shape over service quality. The T13 rule generalizes: a number is comparable only inside its slice and its window.

The same refusal protects the fairness audit: "tenant B was rejected 30 times over the audited window by the per-tenant envelope while its weight was 1.0" is an auditable sentence; "tenant B is the worst tenant" is not a sentence the journal can prove or refute. The runbook's fairness row cites the window and the rule, exactly like every other field — because fairness that cannot cite its window is an accusation, not an audit.

The shift handover for adapters

The adapter era changes what a shift handover must carry, and the extra fields are all ledger fields. The outgoing shift states, per busy slot: the routing policy live (window W), the swap count over the handover window, the per-tenant p95 table with its windows, the adapter digests resident, and any rank experiment in flight with its frozen plan and its stop rule. The incoming shift can then answer the only three questions a handover exists for: is the policy the one the memo approved, are the promises holding on their slices, and is any experiment past its stop rule. Three yeses and the handover is a receipt; any no names the first incident to inspect.

What the handover must not carry is the old era's cargo: a global latency chart (wrong grain), an adapter "quality" banner without a slice (an unauditable claim), or a swap count without its window and policy (a number no one can compare). The handover is a routing memo in miniature — which is why the chapter made the memo one page: if it cannot be handed over in one page, it is not yet a policy but an argument.

Why agents make this worse before better

The topic's forward pointer deserves its own honesty note: agentic workloads arrive burstier, longer and more prefix-dependent than human traffic, so the tenant stream this chapter routed is the calm version of the future stream. Longer sessions mean a tenant's requests hold the slot longer (more head-of-line pressure on pinned queues); tool waits mean arrivals cluster after the wait (windows fill with one tenant's post-tool fan-out); prefix reuse means the cache coupling from the earlier section grows sharper. Every one of those makes the two-sided ledger more expensive per side — which is the chapter's real gift to Topic 15: the instruments (slices, swap counts, the window dial, the two-sided ledger) are exactly what survives the workload's shape change, and the constants will move again.

Migrating a tenant between policies

Policies are not forever: the memo that chose W = 3 was true of the stream it measured, and streams change. Migrating a tenant from FIFO to affinity windows — or from one window to another — is a routing change with its own two-sided price, and it deserves the same freeze-then-measure discipline as any knob. Freeze the migration plan: the new W, the declared lines (swaps and waits), the window over which the comparison runs. Then measure both sides on the old policy's journal window and the new policy's live window, and reconcile them against the simulator — the same prediction-reconciliation the T06 knee and the T10 sweep ran. A migration without the old window's baseline is a policy change nobody can evaluate: the comparison needs both sides named.

Two honest edge cases live at the migration's edges. A tenant mid-window when the policy flips has requests that straddle two policies — the journal must record the policy version per entry (one more field the tenant slice carries) so the comparison slices cleanly. And a tenant whose traffic shape changes between windows has moved the thing being measured — the honest memo says so: "the W = 5 row reflects the burst week" names the confound instead of hiding it. Policy versions, like adapter digests, are release notes for the routing layer.

The chapter in one ledger

Price the sliver: the rank arithmetic makes an adapter 0.22 MB against a 491 MB frozen base, and the rank dial is measured per tenant, never assumed. Count the swaps: they are order-dependent, fivefold between interleaved and grouped on the same load, priced at a declared 2 ms with the real-systems line cited. Measure per tenant: the promise is nearest-rank over the tenant's own journal slice, window named, small-sample caveat stated. Route with both sides priced: the window dial trades waits against swaps, the declared lines make the choice checkable, and the fairness row closes the loop — who was rejected by which rule, over which audited window. Four ledger fields, one page, one simulator to reconcile against. That is the chapter; everything after it moves the constants.
