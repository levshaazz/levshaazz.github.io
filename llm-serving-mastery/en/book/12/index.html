Advanced Kubernetes: single-pod concurrency & HPA

One pod already serves a crowd — continuous batching holds the GPU at 90 % with an empty queue, so utilization is the anti-signal, and queue depth, KV pressure and TTFT are the honest signals. The HPA is one formula — desired = ceil(replicas × metric / target), clamped — guarded by an inclusive ±10 % tolerance band and asymmetric windows: scale-up now, scale-down after 300 s of maximum recommendations. A new pod pays the 165 s boot, so scale-down is cautious; the failure drill counts what one lost pod costs.

One pod is already a crowd

Topic 11 ended with a fleet that declares itself: pods sized from the ledger, boots budgeted by probes, releases kept as append-only history. One number in those manifests is still a human decision wearing a default: replicas. You chose 3. Why 3? Because it looked reasonable. And that is the last honest reason a human should choose it — because the honest answer changes through the day. The morning trickle needs one pod; the noon burst needs six; the night needs whatever cheap is. A static number is wrong in both directions at once: idle replicas burn GPU-hours (real money, priced in Topic 13), and a burst that outgrows the fleet produces the Topic 8 outcome — 24 arrivals met an envelope built for fewer, and the extras got honest 429s.

The HorizontalPodAutoscaler — HPA — is the controller that makes that decision continuously, inside the cluster, from a metric you choose. Its entire arithmetic is one line, and one of this chapter's purposes is to earn respect for that line: by the end you will compute the decision by hand, guard it with a tolerance band and two stabilization windows, price what a wrong decision costs, and rehearse the whole loop in the lab's simulator, HPASim.

The other purpose is the signal. The naive instinct — "the GPU is busy, add pods" — is wrong for LLM serving in a way that is worth internalising deeply, because it looks right and fails quietly. The chapter's first figure is that failure in one picture; everything after it is the correction.

The anti-signal: GPU at 90 %

Continuous batching — Topic 5's machinery — fills the engine's slots with concurrent requests so the GPU does useful work on many sequences per step. The consequence for scaling is counterintuitive: a well-served LLM engine keeps its GPU busy at loads where a naive watcher would panic. Utilization near 90 % is what a healthy engine looks like at half capacity, because the slots are full and the step is packed — utilization reports the efficiency of the batching, not the pressure of the demand.

Read the figure as the topic's thesis in one window. The amber line — GPU utilization — is flat at 90 % before, during and after the burst: batching absorbed the extra arrivals without moving the needle you were tempted to watch. The blue line — queue depth per pod — is the honest one: zero until the burst, five per pod after, and it is the number the controller should read. The llm-d KubeCon NA 2026 talk compresses it into the sentence this course adopts: raw GPU utilization tells you very little about an LLM service. Posttest p2 asks you to say why, and the answer is that sentence plus one mechanism: batching.

Hold the negative conclusion as firmly as the positive one: "GPU is hot" is not evidence of overload, and scaling on it adds booting pods that serve nothing while the real signal — the queue — is empty. Exercise 7 makes you write the refusal argument in two sentences, because you will need it in a design review, not in an exam.

The signals that do not lie

Three serving-derived signals carry the truth about demand. Queue depth per pod — how many requests are waiting at the door after the slots filled — is the most direct: it is un-served demand, measured where it accumulates. KV-cache pressure — how full the context memory is — is the Topic 02 scarcity as a gauge: near saturation, new requests will be refused or evicted no matter how idle the compute looks.TTFT — time to first token — is the client-visible promise bending under load; it is what the SLO of Topic 13 will be written about. The lab uses queue depth as its teaching signal because its arithmetic is the cleanest; the others enter as vocabulary and as the production practice of the later sections.

The catch is plumbing, and it is worth naming so the lab's arithmetic stays honest about itself: the stock HPA reads CPU and memory — container metrics that say nothing about serving demand. To scale on queue depth, the metric has to be exported and fed in: the Prometheus Adapter wires custom metrics into the stock HPA; KEDA offers event-driven scalers (and scale-to-zero); llm-d's WVA weighs several serving signals into one desired-replica verdict. Different plumbing, one arithmetic — which is why the course trains the arithmetic and names the plumbing.

A note on honesty that the lab keeps in its footer: queue depth "per pod" needs a denominator that only exists after pods exist, and at one pod the number is noisy. The declared teaching model uses it anyway because the controller shape is what is being taught; a production design would smooth it, aggregate it, or switch to a weighted verdict — the llm-d approach — and declare that choice too.

The HPA formula

Compute it once by hand and the shape is yours forever. Replicas 3, queue 5 per pod, target 2: ratio 2.5, desired = ceil(3 × 2.5) = ceil(7.5) = 8, clamp [2, 10] leaves 8. The three parts do three different jobs, and each has a failure mode the lab's hidden tests hunt. Without the ceiling — int(7.5) = 7 — you quietly under-provision against fractional demand, which is exactly what bursts produce. Without the clamp, a metric bug (a doubling of the reported queue) rents the entire node pool at today's GPU prices. Without the ratio's named target, the whole thing is unmetered vibes.

One reading habit the course insists on: the formula computes a recommendation, not an action. What stands between the recommendation and the fleet are the guards — the band that can refuse to act at all, and the windows that can delay acting on the way down. The next two sections are those guards.

Three guards: band, ceil, clamp

Guards exist because measurements are noisy and actions are expensive. The band— the tolerance — decides whether the recommendation deserves action at all: if the ratio sits inside ±10 % of 1.0, the current fleet size is within the promise's tolerance, and the correct decision is to do nothing. The ceiling decides how to act when action is due: always up, because a fraction of a pod's worth of demand still needs a whole pod. Theclamp decides the boundaries of the possible: minReplicas is the floor the fleet never scales below, maxReplicas is the fuse above which no metric, however broken, may push.

Walk the decision tree as the lab's walkthrough does: measure the queue; compute the ratio; if it is inside the band — hold, done; otherwise ceil, clamp, and that is the new desired count — which the controller then requests, with the actual pod creation still subject to scheduling and the 165 s boot. The guards sit before the expensive part, and their whole job is to make sure the expensive part fires rarely and honestly.

The number to memorise from this section is the band's: ±10 %, inclusive on both edges. The number to understand from this section is why it is inclusive — and that deserves its own section, because it is the thermostat.

The tolerance band: the thermostat

A thermostat that fires at every 0.1 degree of deviation clicks on and off all night and wears itself out. The HPA's band is the same device: between 0.9 and 1.1 times the target, the fleet is doing its job well enough that changing its size costs more than the deviation does. The inclusive edges are the whole subtlety, and quiz q2 hunts them: at ratio exactly 1.1 the correct answer is hold, because an exclusive edge would make a constant, parked ratio oscillate — scale on one tick, hold on the next — which is flapping, the disease the band exists to prevent. The follow-up asks for that reason in words, not for the number.

The cost of a missing band is worth pricing once. Without it, every tick near the target is a scale event; each scale-up boots pods that pay 165 s before serving; each scale-down removes capacity that the next tick's noise asks for back. The fleet churns, the bill oscillates, and the SLO dies of indecision rather than of load. Hysteresis — the dead band — is the cheapest reliability mechanism in the chapter: it is free, and it is the difference between a controller and a mood.

Up now, down carefully

The asymmetry is risk pricing, and both prices come from things you have already measured. Scaling up too late costs 429s and bent TTFTs immediately — the burst does not wait. Scaling down too early costs the same at the next burst, plus a boot per new pod: every capacity you release may have to be re-earned 165 seconds later, with clients queued while it loads weights. Hence: up is instant (window 0 s), down waits, and while it waits it reads themaximum of its trailing recommendations — one spike inside the window holds the fleet at the spike's size for the full 300 s, because one quiet minute is not evidence that the demand is gone.

The 300 s and the 15 s tick are Kubernetes' defaults, rounded into the lab's model as exactly 20 ticks — and the rounding is declared, like every model constant in this course. The number 20 matters twice: it is the window's length in the arithmetic, and it is the hidden edge in ex3 — a window of exactly 20 ticks counts as the window, and a peak 19 ticks ago still holds the replicas. Quiz q3 asks for the division (300/15 = 20) with its why attached: the window remembers the worst recent advice, because the worst recent advice is the only proven capacity requirement on the table.

The window in one picture

The figure is ex3's bug and fix side by side over a spiky window: spike, quiet gap, second spike. The red line is the naive controller — follow the latest recommendation instantly. It climbs to 4 with the first spike, collapses to 1 in the quiet gap (instant down, no window), and then serves the second burst from one pod while the others boot: the SLO dies in the gap, exactly as the figure's annotation says. The green line is the windowed controller: it also climbs to 4, and then holds 4 — the maximum of its trailing twenty recommendations — until the quiet is old enough to be believed.

Price the difference honestly: the green line pays for four pods through the quiet gap; the red line pays less between the spikes and catastrophically more at the second one. The window is not conservatism — it is the arithmetic of "capacity you released has to be re-earned with a boot". The lab's fix-the-bug starter implements the red line, the students implement the green, and the hidden tests pin the two edges: the exactly-20-tick window and the peak that holds.

The boot makes down cautious

Why does the down direction need a window at all? Because of the number this course has carried since the fleet ran: 165 s. Every pod whose capacity you release must be re-earned, on the next burst, by a boot of that length — extract, weights, init, health — with clients queued behind it. The Topic 11 startup probe budgeted that boot for one pod becoming Ready; this chapter prices it into the controller's memory. The scale-down window's 300 s is, in this reading, several boots' worth of caution — the cluster refusing to bet the fleet on one quiet minute when re-armament takes 165 s.

The figure is Topic 9's startup economics; it returns here as the price tag on elasticity. The extract member, the weights delivery, the init and the health check — the same members the startup probe waits for — are what a scale-down saves and a scale-up re-pays. This is also the honest reason big-model fleets rarely scale to zero: the first client of the next burst pays the whole boot alone, in cold latency. KEDA's scale-to-zero is wonderful for small models and spiky traffic, and painful exactly in proportion to the boot — the vocabulary from the practice section, priced by this one.

Fix the bug: the flutter

Ex3 is the topic's fix-the-bug rung, and its starter is plausible code: a controller that reads the latest recommendation and applies it immediately. On a smooth metric it behaves. On a sawtooth — the shape of real traffic — it flaps: 1 ↔ 2 ↔ 1 on successive ticks. Two costs, both visible in the lab's report. The SLO dies on every down: each collapse is capacity removed while demand still oscillates. And the bill jerks: every up boots pods (165 s each), every down throws away warmed engines — churn that shows up in Topic 13's ledger as wasted GPU-hours.

The fix is one line and one discipline: the down direction reads the maximum recommendation over the trailing 300 s (20 ticks); the up direction stays instant. The hidden tests make you honest about the edges: a window of exactly 20 ticks counts; a peak inside the window holds the replicas; the quiet between spikes proves nothing until it is twenty ticks old. When the fix passes, run the lab's report once more and read the two numbers it now shows — held ticks and unserved ticks — because that pair is the SLO's diary, and it is what the milestone asks you to write about.

The transfer worth naming: hysteresis in the autoscaler is the same device as debounce in a UI, the dead band in a thermostat, and the admission envelope of Topic 8 — decisions separated from noise by an explicit, named gap. You have now met the pattern in four costumes; recognize it the fifth time without being told.

HPASim: the closed loop

The capstone class assembles the whole chapter into a simulator that runs in the browser.HPASim(w_ms=60, b_ms=2, cap=30, target_queue=2, window_ticks=20) — the knobs are the T6 step model (step time 60 + 2 ms per sequence, batch ceiling 30) plus this chapter's controller constants. tick(arrivals) delivers a wave of requests and returns the tick's verdict: the queue per pod, the band check, the decision, the fleet's serving rate, and the latency judgement against the working point. report() sums the run: held ticks, unserved ticks, and the trajectory of the fleet size.

The figure is the step-time economics from Topic 10's model family: the same declared knobs (W = 60 ms of weight-streaming work per step, b = 2 ms per sequence, a batch ceiling of 30) that produce the working point of 250 tok/s per pod. The loop closes through the queue, and Topic 6's Little's law is visible inside one simulator: slow the service (a kill), and the queue grows; the growing queue trips the band; the controller scales up; the boot elapses; the queue drains. Nothing in that sentence is magic — it is the formula, the band, the windows and the capacity line running in order, which is exactly what the star challenge then asks you to tune.

The failure drill

The milestone's third artifact is incident notes, and the lab's drill gives them their numbers. Four replicas at the working point promise 1000 tok/s. Kill one: the line drops to 750 tok/s and stays there for the replacement's boot — 165 s of 250 tok/s shortfall, the number exercise 5 computes. The same drill at the floor — minReplicas 1 — is the honest disaster: the single pod dies, the Service has no endpoints, and clients meet a 503 window that lasts scheduling plus boot, with zero capacity behind it.

Two vocabulary words guard the drill's edges, and posttest-adjacent questions like exercise 6 separate them cleanly. minReplicas is the autoscaler's floor: the HPA itself will never scale below it, so at 2 a single kill leaves a serving pod behind — degraded, not dark. PodDisruptionBudget is the drain's floor: it bounds voluntary disruptions (node upgrades, cluster maintenance — how many pods may be taken at once), and it does not protect against crashes. The crash protection is the replica count itself; confusing the two words is how teams discover that their PDB was never a safety net.

Write the incident notes in the drill's grammar — what broke, what it cost in tok/s, how long the window lasted, which knob would have blunted it — because that grammar is the milestone's format and, in spirit, every postmortem you will ever file. The numbers are small on purpose: an incident you can hold in your head teaches the shape of every incident you won't.

Admission and autoscaling: two envelopes

Topic 8 built the admission envelope — the honest 429: when arrivals exceed what the slots and queue can hold, the gateway refuses the extras immediately, with a reason. The HPA is the second envelope, and the two compose rather than compete. Admission protects the fleetnow — instant, per-request, no boot involved. Autoscaling restores and extends the envelope over minutes — it pays boots, it waits for windows. A burst that fits meets a fleet that absorbs it; a burst that exceeds meets 429s at the door and a fleet growing behind the wall; sustained demand meets a bigger steady fleet with the admission envelope resized around it.

The figure is Topic 8's queue economy; reread it here and the composition is visible: the queue in front of the engines is the same queue the HPA reads. When the queue is honest and the envelope is honest, the controller's inputs are honest — garbage in any layer and the autoscaler scales a fiction. That is why this course built the layers in order: the journal (T08) made the queue provable, the probes (T11) made Ready honest, and only now does a controller get to act on them.

The rehearsal the lab walks in prose — no runtime needed — repeats the T8 burst on paper: 24 arrivals against the old envelope, 16 honest 429s, then a hand "HPA tick" (you, playing the controller, doubling the replicas in the compose file), and the same burst again with fewer refusals. Do it once on paper and the division of labour is permanent: the envelope absorbs seconds, the autoscaler absorbs minutes, and neither pretends to be the other.

What the wrong decision costs

Both directions of a wrong replicas have a price, and the course prices them with Topic 13's declared arithmetic — cost per million output tokens = price × 1,000,000 / (throughput × 3600) — so the numbers here are quotes, not measurements. Over-provisioningpays for idle GPUs by the hour: two replicas above the demand line, at a declared 2.00 per GPU-hour, burn 4.00 per hour while serving nothing extra — 96 per day for a fleet size chosen by vibes. Under-provisioning pays in the currency of Topic 8: honest 429s and bent TTFTs, which the SLO of Topic 13 converts into a breach with a timestamp. Andflapping pays in boots: every oscillation loads weights that are thrown away on the next down-tick — 165 s of disk and GPU work per churned pod, multiplied by however many times the missing band let the controller twitch.

The pricing habit matters more than any single number, because it turns the guards of this chapter into line items. The tolerance band stops paying boots for noise. The clamp stops a metric bug from renting the node pool at full price. The scale-down window stops one quiet minute from triggering a fire-and-refill cycle. When Topic 13 asks you to build a cost ledger with named levers, you will discover you already own three: they were the guards, all along, and each one exists because the unguarded version of the same controller is measurably more expensive.

One boundary sentence, in the course's grammar: these are declared prices on a teaching fleet — the 2.00 per GPU-hour is the same declared tier T13 uses, and the throughput line is this topic's 250 tok/s per pod. The exercise for the reader is to redo the three computations with the prices of a real quote they have at hand; the shape of the argument — guards are line items — survives every price list unchanged. And when the price list arrives, the ledger habit from Topic 10 applies to it verbatim: itemise the rows, name the knobs, and refuse any total whose components you cannot point to.

Practice: what prod scales on

The plumbing has three names worth carrying. The Prometheus Adapter exports serving metrics (queue depth, KV pressure, TTFT) as custom metrics the stock HPA can read — the minimal-production path for the arithmetic you already know. KEDA adds event-driven scalers — queue lengths from brokers, streams, cron — and the scale-to-zero trick, attractive for small models and spiky traffic, painful in exact proportion to the boot.llm-d's WVA weighs several serving signals into one desired-replica verdict — the "weighted verdict" approach for fleets that trust no single gauge. Around them, the vLLM production stack pairs the autoscaler with a KV-cache-aware router, so that scale events land on warmed pods instead of cold ones.

The stable core under all four names is the skeleton you have built: a ratio, a band, a ceiling, a clamp, windows. When a design review produces a fifth name, ask for the skeleton — what is the metric, the target, the band, the fuse, the memory? A scaler that cannot answer those five questions is a mood, and the course has taught you to price moods in GPU-hours.

One more honest boundary: everything in this section is cited practice, not executed practice — the lab's environment has no Prometheus, no KEDA, no cluster, and it says so. The arithmetic is executable in your browser; the plumbing is named so that the first time you meet it in production, it is a re-acquaintance, not a revelation.

The T03 bridge: quantize the boot

The bridge the posttest drills, in prose: switch the fleet to an INT4 engine. What changes? The weights member of the boot shrinks (roughly half the bytes to load), the per-pod ledger row shrinks (more replicas fit per node), and the cold scale-up's hidden cost — the 165 s of physics between a scale decision and usable capacity — shrinks with it. Elasticity got cheaper; the fleet can afford to be more elastic at the same SLO.

What does not change: the policy. The HPA's formula, band and windows never mention weight precision — desired = ceil(replicas × ratio), clamped, is as true of INT4 as of fp16. The layer boundary is worth stating precisely because this course drew the same one in Topic 3: quantization changes the cost of things; the controller decides about quantities. A quantized fleet scales with the same skeleton, from the same queue, against the same promise — and the runbook's one honest edit is to re-measure the boot and re-budget the startup probe before trusting any quoted second.

Exercise 8 makes you write that boundary sentence under a deliberately uncertain number — "roughly half" is a declared proportion, not a measured boot — because the course's evidence grammar applies to bridges twice over: the mechanism may be certain, and the seconds still need provenance.

Reveal eight answers and their limits
  1. Run the formula by hand. Ratio = 5/2 = 2.5; desired = ceil(3 × 2.5) = ceil(7.5) = 8; clamp [2, 10] leaves 8. With maxReplicas 8 the clamp still leaves 8 — the formula and the fuse agree. The ceiling matters before the clamp because rounding down would quietly refuse demand: int(7.5) = 7 pods against demand worth 7.5 replicas is a shortage you chose. The order is ceil first, clamp second — the lab's hidden tests reject both the int() version and a clamp-before-ceil ordering.
  2. The inclusive edges of the band. Ratio 0.90 is inside [0.9, 1.1] — hold. Ratio 1.10 is exactly the edge — still hold: the band is inclusive on both sides, and the lab's quiz q2 pins exactly this case. Ratio 1.11 is outside — ceil(2 × 1.11) = ceil(2.22) = 3 replicas. The edges are inclusive because a metric parked exactly at ±10 % must not flap the fleet: an exclusive edge turns a constant ratio into an oscillating decision — scale, hold, scale — which is the thermostat clicking on and off all night. The dead band exists to absorb noise; inclusive edges are what make it a band.
  3. The int() that starved the fleet. The buggy version: int(2 × 2.5) = int(5.0) = 5 — here the ratio divides evenly, so this case passes and hides the bug. Change the condition by one: queue 6 per pod → ratio 3.0, still exact. Queue 5 with replicas 3: 3 × 2.5 = 7.5 → int gives 7, ceil gives 8. The missing pod is real demand refused: seven pods serve what eight were computed for, and the queue stays longer. The bug is silent precisely when it matters — fractional ratios, which is what bursts produce. That is why ex1's hidden tests feed fractional cases.
  4. The exactly-twenty-tick edge. The window covers ticks 5 through 24 inclusive — twenty ticks. At every tick in that range the maximum recommendation inside the window is 4, so the controller holds 4. At tick 25 the spike leaves the window: the max over the remaining ticks is the quiet recommendation, and scale-down becomes possible. While the window slides, the controller takes the maximum of the trailing recommendations — not the latest. A peak inside the window holds the replicas for the full 300 s; that is ex3's hidden edge, and it is what keeps one quiet minute from stranding the fleet.
  5. Count what the kill cost. Before: 4 × 250 = 1000 tok/s. During the boot: 3 × 250 = 750 tok/s — a loss of 250 tok/s for 165 s, i.e. about 41,250 tokens of lost service versus the promised line. The loss is bounded by the boot because Kubernetes replaces the pod automatically: the Deployment notices the dead member, schedules a replacement, and the startup probe gates it into the Service exactly when the weights land. The same kill at minReplicas 1 with one pod is unbounded in a different way — there is nothing behind the dead pod, so the loss lasts the whole boot at 100 % of traffic (the 503 window).
  6. The floor under the floor. With one pod, the OOMKill is a full outage: the Service has no endpoints, so clients see connection failures (in the lab's model, the 503 window) for scheduling plus the boot — 165 s of physics with zero capacity behind it. minReplicas 2 changes the arithmetic completely: the HPA never scales below two, so a single kill leaves a serving pod behind — degraded, not dark. A PodDisruptionBudget does NOT help here: PDB constrains voluntary disruptions (drains, upgrades — how many pods may be taken at once), not crashes. The crash protection is the replica count itself.
  7. Choose the signal, refuse the mood. On the honest signal the decision is: ratio = 0/2 = 0, inside the band [0.9, 1.1] — hold; no scale event at all. The argument: GPU utilization at 85 % with an empty queue is continuous batching doing its job — the slots are full, the engine is efficient, and demand is fully served with nothing waiting. Scaling up would add pods that boot for 165 s and then sit idle, burning GPU-hours (Topic 13 prices them) for capacity nobody asked for. The queue is the demand signal; utilization is an efficiency report.
  8. Quantize the boot, keep the policy. The policy changes nothing: desired = ceil(replicas × metric / target), the inclusive ±10 % band and the 0/300 s windows are untouched — the controller never knew the weight precision. The economics change: a smaller boot makes scale-up useful sooner (the new pod's capacity arrives earlier), and more replicas per node raise the ceiling that maxReplicas and the node pool impose. The exact seconds are honestly uncertain — the 165 s is the fp16 fleet's measured boot, and "roughly half the weights member" is a declared proportion, not a measured boot; the runbook would re-budget the startup probe against the new engine's measured median before trusting any number.
