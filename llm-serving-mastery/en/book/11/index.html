Kubernetes for LLM serving: GPU Operator & Helm

You already run a fleet — compose engines with limits, a hash-chained journal, shard replicas. Kubernetes turns that by-hand loop into declared state: the pod inherits the ledger (16+4)/p + 1.5 GiB and the 165 s cold start, the startupProbe budgets the boot (5 × 40 = 200 s ≥ 165 s), a rolling update with maxUnavailable 0 holds the capacity floor, and a Helm release is append-only history where rollback creates a new revision. The GPU dictionary — Operator, time-slicing, MIG — is honest vocabulary; the lab is a declared teaching model of a cluster, not your cluster.

The loop you already run

By the end of Topic 10 you were operating a small fleet by hand. A compose file from Topic 08 declared a gateway and llama.cpp engines with mem_limit ceilings; Topic 09 taught you what the limit means when the weights are resident (exit 137, and a restart policy that tries again); Topic 10 put shard replicas behind one address and made the fit decision from an itemised ledger. But look at what the operation itself still is: you watch, you notice the dead engine, you type the restart command, you decide when to roll the new image, and you hold the capacity math in your head while you do it.

That loop works at three engines. At thirty it does not — not because any step gets harder, but because the loop does not scale with the fleet: someone must watch liveness at 3 a.m., someone must roll the new image engine by engine while the clients keep arriving, and someone must know, at every moment, how much capacity the fleet has while weights are loading. Kubernetes is the decision to stop running that loop by hand. It is not a new engine and not a new physics — it is a new relationship to the fleet you already have, and every artifact in this chapter is part of that relationship.

The chapter keeps the course's honesty rules in force. The numbers you will size pods with are the ones you already measured or computed: the ledger (16 + 4)/p + 1.5 GiB is Topic 10's, and the 165 s cold start is the measured boot of the T09 fleet run. When the chapter says "the cluster waits 200 seconds for a pod", that is declared arithmetic about a teaching model — and the lab says so in its intro and its footer, the same way the Topic 10 lab said "declared CPU simulation".

Desired state, not commands

The inversion at the heart of Kubernetes is small to state and large to internalise: you do not tell the cluster what to do; you tell it what should exist, and controllers keep making the world match the claim. A Deployment is a declaration — replicas 3, a pod template with resource requests and limits, probes with budgets — and a reconciliation loop continuously compares the declared state to the observed state and repairs the difference. kubectl apply is not an action; it is a claim filed with a system whose job is to make the claim true.

Why this suits LLM serving specifically: everything you learned so far was already declarative in spirit. The ledger is a claim about what a pod needs; the probe budget is a claim about how long the boot takes; the admission envelope of Topic 08 is a claim about how many requests the fleet will accept. Kubernetes gives those claims a format (YAML validated by a schema) and an executor (the controllers). The lab's simulators consume the same shapes —requests == limits, probe periods and thresholds, rollout parameters — so what you declare in the browser is what you would declare to a real cluster, minus the cluster.

One more property matters for everything that follows: reconciliation is continuous. A crashed pod is not an incident that pages you; it is a difference between declared and observed state that the controller repairs on its next pass. The restart loop you built by hand in Topic 09 — restart until /health answers — is, in Kubernetes, somebody else's loop: the Deployment's backoff, driven by the declaration. Your job moves from doing the repairs to writing the declarations that make repairs unnecessary.

The pod: your container's contract, renamed

A pod is the smallest deployable unit: one or more containers that share an address and a lifecycle. For this course the pod is the member container from Topic 09 with its contract renamed into Kubernetes vocabulary. The cgroup ceiling you set with mem_limit 1200mbecomes resources.limits.memory; crossing it is still OOMKilled, still exit 137 at the container level, and the restart is still there — now as the Deployment's restart policy with its backoff, not your hand on the keyboard.

What the rename adds is the second number. Alongside limits — the runtime ceiling — Kubernetes asks for requests: the amount the scheduler promises to the pod when deciding which node it fits on. The pair is the pod's negotiating position: requests are what the scheduler reserves, limits are what the runtime enforces. When requests equal limits, the pod is declaring "what I reserved is exactly what I will use" — and that equality is the whole next section.

Keep the physics pinned while the vocabulary moves: the weights are resident and non-negotiable; the KV cache grows with the work; the fixed overhead of the runtime does not shrink because you wished. Nothing about a pod makes memory cheaper. What a pod offers is a schedulable, replaceable, addressable unit whose appetite is declared in numbers the scheduler can read — which is exactly the ledger you already keep, in a format a machine can finally consume.

The ledger becomes a manifest

Topic 10's ledger makes the jump intact: weights 16 GiB and KV cache 4 GiB shard by the tensor-parallel degree, the fixed 1.5 GiB does not, and the per-pod memory is (16 + 4)/p + 1.5 GiB. At p = 1 the pod declares 21.5 GiB; at p = 2, 11.5 GiB; at p = 4, 6.5 GiB. The manifest rows are the ledger rows — nothing new is being computed, only formatted for a scheduler to read:

Written into a manifest, the p = 2 row reads: resources.requests.memory: 11.5Gi,resources.limits.memory: 11.5Gi, resources.limits.nvidia.com/gpu: "2". The GPU line is vocabulary for now — slide fifteen makes it precise — but its shape already says the important thing: a GPU is a schedulable quantity exactly like memory, counted by the scheduler, requested and limited like anything else.

The skill to carry out of this section is that sizing is not folklore. "Which degree fits which node" is a row in a table, computed the same way in Topic 10's stretch card and in this chapter's manifest: the sharded part divides, the fixed part stays, and the answer either fits the budget or the service does not start. A scheduler can only be as honest as the numbers it is given — under-declare and the pod lands where it cannot survive; over-declare and the cluster reserves capacity the pod never uses.

The figure above is the ledger you already know; the manifest is that table wearing a schema. The one new failure mode the format introduces is a transcription error — declaring 1.5 GiB instead of 11.5 GiB — and the failure surfaces not at write time but at the first OOMKill, which is why the lab's hidden tests check the equality of requests and limits rather than trusting the shape of the YAML.

QoS: Guaranteed by arithmetic

Kubernetes sorts every pod into one of three quality-of-service classes, and the sorting rule is pure arithmetic. Guaranteed: requests equal limits on every container in the pod. Burstable: at least one container where requests sit below limits — cheap to schedule, first to be squeezed. BestEffort: no requests or limits at all — first out, always. The classes exist because nodes run out of memory, and when they do, the eviction order follows the classes: BestEffort pods die first, Burstable next, Guaranteed last.

For an LLM pod the choice is mechanical, and the lab's hidden test enforces it by rejectingrequests < limits outright. The weights are resident; the engine cannot "grow into" a bigger limit the way a stateless web service might, because the memory it needs on second one is the memory it needs on day thirty. Under-promise and the scheduler will co-locate a neighbour the pod cannot survive next to; when the node runs hot, the eviction order decides who dies — and a Burstable LLM pod is evictable by design.

The exam voice for this is posttest p1: what separates Guaranteed from Burstable is not a bigger number, it is the equality — requests equal limits on every container. The deeper point is the one the class system encodes: in a shared cluster, your declaration is other people's reality. The numbers you write decide where the scheduler may put you and who gets evicted when the node runs out. Guaranteed is not a luxury tier; it is the honest declaration of a pod whose appetite is known to the GiB.

TP degree = pod size

The ledger table hides a sentence worth making explicit: the tensor-parallel degree of the engine inside the pod determines the pod's size and its GPU count. At p = 1 the pod asks for one GPU and 21.5 GiB; at p = 2, two GPUs and 11.5 GiB; at p = 4, four GPUs and 6.5 GiB. The scaling axis you studied inside one machine in Topic 10 becomes, in the cluster vocabulary, theshape of the schedulable unit.

The mechanism does not change: the weights split column- and row-parallel with all-reduce beats at the seams, and the beats demand a fast interconnect inside one node. What changes is who arranges the pieces. In Topic 10 you assembled the shards by hand in NumPy and proved the join exact; in a cluster, the engine inside the pod does the splitting, and the pod declares the resulting appetite — memory and GPU count together — as its requests. The proof discipline carries over as a deployment discipline: a pod whose engine cannot serve (weights missing, shards mismatched) must not become Ready, which is precisely the probe contract two sections ahead.

This is also the right place to note what a pod is not: it is not a machine. Four pods at p = 2 are not "an 8-GPU machine"; they are four schedulable units, each demanding two GPUs and 11.5 GiB, each booting independently, each paying its own cold start. The fleet-of-pods view is what makes the rest of the chapter work — rolling updates replace pods one at a time, Services route across Ready pods, and the HPA in Topic 12 adds or removes whole pods. Everything scales in units of pods, and the pod's size is the ledger's business.

Service: one address over changing pods

Pods are cattle: each has an IP that dies with it. Clients cannot talk to pods directly, for the same reason Topic 08's clients did not talk to engines directly — the thing that changes must not be the thing the client addresses. A Service is the stable virtual address and a selector: it watches the fleet for pods carrying its labels, and routes to those that areReady. The endpoint list grows and shrinks as pods come and go; the client sees one name.

This is the same shape as the gateway from Topics 08–09 — one honest front door, engines behind it — promoted to cluster vocabulary. And it introduces the word this whole chapter turns on: Ready. The endpoint list tracks readiness, not liveness and not "container started". A pod that is Up but not Ready receives zero traffic; a pod that is Ready receives its share. For a stateless microservice, Ready is a formality checked in milliseconds. For an LLM pod, whose boot replays extract → weights → init → /health, Ready is 165 seconds of physics — and the next three sections give that word a definition precise enough to build on.

Rolling updates are safe only because of this arrangement: the new pod is added to the endpoint list when (and only when) it is Ready; the old pod leaves the list before it dies; the client never addresses a pod at all. Hold that thought — the rolling-update section will come back to it and show exactly what happens to fleet capacity when the plan is written carelessly.

The boot is physics: 165 seconds

The T09 fleet run measured the boot of a member container end to end: extract the image layers, load the weights, initialise the engine, answer /health. For this course's declared 8B teaching model the number attached to that sequence is 165 seconds — a course-inherited constant, quoted from seminars/runs/2026-11-07-topic09-fleet-mac/, not invented for this topic. In Docker you watched that number once and arranged your work around it. In a cluster, every pod boot pays it — every scale-up, every replacement, every rolling update — which is why the next hour of this chapter is about teaching the cluster to wait.

Read the figure as a contract between two numbers. The blue bar is the boot: 165 seconds before the pod can serve its first token. The green bar is the cluster's patience: the startupProbe budget, whose arithmetic is the next section's whole content. The 35 seconds between them are the headroom — and the headroom is not slack. The 165 s is a median measured on one machine; the node that will host your pod next year has a different disk, and the budget must cover the tail of the boot distribution, not its median. Quiz q2's follow-up asks exactly this: why plan for more than the median? Because the median says nothing about the pod that lands on the slow node, and that pod is the one that dies if you sized the patience to the average.

Three probes, three jobs

Kubernetes wires three probes into a pod spec, and they answer three different questions. The startupProbe answers "has it finished booting?" — it runs first, and liveness and readiness are suspended until it passes once. The readinessProbeanswers "should it receive traffic now?" — failing it removes the pod from the Service's endpoint list while the pod itself keeps living. The livenessProbe answers "is it alive at all?" — failing it kills and restarts the container.

The LLM-specific discipline is keeping those questions separate. Generation latency routinely exceeds naive probe timeouts, so a liveness probe tuned for a web service will murder a healthy but busy engine — and killing it throws away the KV cache and the queued work, converting "slow" into "cold". The rule the lab encodes: a slow generation must fail readiness (stop sending new traffic to this pod) and must never fail liveness (do not restart a working engine for being busy). Posttest p2 drills exactly this distinction, and the OpenAI KubeCon talk this chapter cites reports the production incidents that come from conflating the two.

The startup probe is the boot's designated waiter. Because liveness and readiness hang until it passes, the cluster's patience for the boot is expressed in exactly one place — its budget — and that is deliberate: one knob, one multiplication, auditable. The next section does the multiplication.

The probe budget: one multiplication

Read the formula as a policy, not an arithmetic exercise. Too small a budget and the cluster kills pods mid-boot, forever — the T09 crash-loop reborn at cluster scale, with the Deployment's backoff as the metronome. Too large a budget and a genuinely dead pod squats its GPU and its memory reservation for minutes before anyone gives up on it. The lab's plan — 200 s against a 165 s median — is the deliberate compromise: 21 % over the median, big enough for a slow disk, small enough that a dead pod does not hold resources for five minutes.

This is also the first place the chapter's numbers meet the grader. The hidden tests of ex2 reject the plan whose budget is 100 s (5 × 20 — less than the boot), reject the plan that compares strictly (165 < 165 is false, but the pod passes exactly at the line), and reject the classic off-by-one in which the first probe "doesn't count". The arithmetic is one multiplication; the discipline is that every knob has a why, and the follow-up quiz demands the why in words: headroom over the median, because the median is not the tail.

Rolling update: hold the floor

Now put the pieces together into the operation that makes Kubernetes worth its vocabulary: replacing the fleet's pods with a new version, in place, without stopping service. The plan is two numbers. maxSurge — how many pods above replicas may exist during the update (how fast new versions arrive). maxUnavailable — how many pods belowreplicas may be missing (how much capacity you are willing to lose). The floor — the guaranteed serving capacity — is replicas − maxUnavailable, and the two plans differ in exactly one of the numbers:

The plan maxUnavailable 0, maxSurge 1 never lets capacity leave 3: a fourth pod starts, boots, passes its startup probe, joins the Service — and only then does one old pod retire. The client never notices. The plan maxUnavailable 2, maxSurge 0 deletes two pods first and serves on one pod for the full 165 seconds the replacement spends loading weights. Same operation, same flags in the same YAML block — one flag of difference, and the difference is a capacity pit exactly as deep and as long as the boot.

Walk the figure once slowly, because it is the exam. The green line is the safe plan: flat at 3 for the whole window. The red line is the honest disaster: a step down to 1 at the moment the update starts, a plateau for 165 s — the boot — and a step back. Quiz q3 asks which parameter raised the floor and the answer is "none of them — maxSurge never raises the floor"; the follow-up demands the reason: surge adds pods above the target, so the floor's formula never touches it. If you remember one inequality from this chapter, it is floor = replicas − maxUnavailable.

The OpenAI lesson: surge is speed, not capacity

The rolling-update failure mode has a name and a stage. At KubeCon NA 2026, OpenAI's talk "Rolling Out LLM Inference Engines on Kubernetes" reported the production form: an LLM inference rollout that looks healthy on every dashboard — the old pods keep serving, the application answers — while the rollout itself is stuck. Every new pod is booting; the first ones never became Ready, so none of the old ones may die; the deployment has been "one unavailable replica" for an hour. Nothing is on fire, which is exactly why nobody paged.

The mechanism behind the stuck rollout is almost always the boot: a probe budget smaller than the real cold start, so each new pod is killed mid-load and restarts into the same trap. The stateless playbook — "replace pods; they come up in seconds" — silently assumes a boot that an LLM engine does not have. The lesson the course takes has two halves. First, the diagnosis: "application healthy + rollout stuck" is not a contradiction but a signature, and it means read the pod events, not the dashboard. Second, the arithmetic: the fix is the budget of the probe section, not a bigger surge — surge adds booting pods, and booting pods serve nothing.

Cite it as a source, not a measurement: the talk is quoted for its failure taxonomy, the way this course quotes SRE literature for golden signals. The lab's fix-the-bug exercise (ex3) hands you the stuck-rollout shape in miniature — a rollout plan that eats capacity — and asks for the floor as the fix, which is the transferable half of the lesson: state the floor, then choose the flags that honour it.

Helm: a release is history

A chart is a template of the manifest; values are the declared numbers that fill it — the ledger row, the probe budget, the replica count. Installing a chart with values creates arelease, and every helm upgrade with new values stamps a newrevision into the release's history: 1, then 2, then 3. helm history lists them. Nothing is overwritten; the history only grows.

The property that makes this honest is the same one that made the T08 journal trustworthy: append-only. A rollback is not a rewind — helm rollback to revision 1 from a history of [1, 2, 3] creates revision 4 whose content equals revision 1's. The old revisions stay; the new state has a new, permanent name. Posttest p3 drills the exact case, because the intuition "rollback goes back" is wrong in exactly the way that matters for audits.

Why the course insists on this vocabulary: the runbook — this topic's milestone artifact — is written in revision numbers. "If the probe budget is wrong in revision 3, roll back to revision 1; expect revision 4 to exist afterwards; verify with helm history" is an instruction a colleague can execute at 3 a.m. without improvising. A release system whose history rewrote itself would make that sentence meaningless — you could not cite the broken state, because its name would have been recycled. Append-only is what makes the runbook quotable.

The figure is from the next block's topic, and it is here for one reason: the shape. An append-only chain of records, each sealing the last, is the same discipline in the journal (T08), in Helm's revisions (here), and in the dashboards that will consume them (T13). When you meet a system that rewrites its history, you now have a reflex: ask what else it lies about.

ReleaseSim: the drill

The capstone class of lab 11 compresses the whole topic into one object with three methods.ReleaseSim(chart) — the constructor installs with initial values and stamps revision 1. upgrade(values) — appends the next revision; called without values, it inherits the previous revision's (a hidden test pins this). rollback(rev) — appends a new revision whose content is the named revision's; called with an unknown revision, it raisesValueError (another hidden test). The class is deliberately small: the entire vocabulary of the topic — install, upgrade, history, rollback — is three methods, and every hidden test encodes one sentence of this chapter.

The drill the lab walks, and the one to rehearse mentally: install with the ledger's values (revision 1); upgrade to a better probe budget (revision 2); make it wrong — a budget of 120 s against a 165 s boot (revision 3, broken); roll back to revision 1 (revision 4 appears, with revision 1's content); verify — history is [1, 2, 3, 4], and the running values match revision 1's. Notice what the broken revision cost: nothing permanent. History absorbed the mistake, the rollback named the good state, and the fleet never served from the broken one — because the broken budget would have crash-looped the pods, which is exactly the kind of mistake you want to make against a simulator first.

The transfer habit to build: after any release operation, state the history out loud. "We are on revision 5; revision 4 was the bad probe budget; rollback to 3 would create revision 6." Operators who can say that sentence from memory do not get lost in incidents; operators who cannot, guess. The lab's journal asks you to write the history after every operation for exactly this reason.

GPU Operator: the dictionary

Everything so far declared memory; the GPU enters as one more schedulable quantity —nvidia.com/gpu: 1 in a pod's limits — and one more piece of machinery that makes the quantity exist: the GPU Operator. Its decomposition is worth memorising as four named pieces, because production incident reports use exactly these words. The driver container loads the NVIDIA kernel driver on every GPU node (a DaemonSet — a pod on every node, which is why you never install drivers by hand). The container-toolkitmakes the container runtime able to hand devices into containers. The device plugin advertises nvidia.com/gpu as a countable resource to the scheduler. The DCGM exporter publishes GPU metrics — the same exporter whose dashboards are the subject of Topic 13.

The honest boundary, stated in the vision doc and repeated here: this course trains the pod-side word and names the machinery; it does not drill a real Operator installation, because there is no GPU at hand and pretending otherwise would be the lie this course does not tell. What transfers without a GPU is the structural insight: a GPU enters the system as a resource with a name, counted and scheduled like memory — and the ledger decides what fits, exactly as it did in Topic 10.

Time-slicing, MIG, MPS

One GPU, several pods — three arrangements with three different trade-offs, and all three are vocabulary in this course, arithmetic in none. Time-slicing: the device plugin advertises several "replicas" of the device, and pods take turns on the hardware. No memory isolation: every slice sees the whole device memory, so one pod's OOM collides with all of them. MIG (on Ampere and newer): the hardware is partitioned into slices with isolated memory and isolated fault domains — a crash stays inside its slice.MPS: multiple processes' work merges into shared GPU contexts — better utilisation than time-slicing, weaker isolation than MIG; the middle word in the dictionary.

The exercise worth doing by hand is the collision of the sharing word with the ledger (exercise 7 below): four 11.5 GiB pods time-slicing one 24 GiB device do not fit, no matter what the YAML says — sharing rotates compute, it does not multiply memory. The habit this builds is the one the course has drilled since Topic 02: when someone proposes a configuration, compute the ledger before admiring the flag. The sharing words change who waits; only the ledger changes what fits.

One sentence on the future, no more: DRA (Dynamic Resource Allocation) reached GA in Kubernetes 1.34 and is the direction these device words are evolving toward — a third-generation API for asking a cluster for accelerators. It changes the plumbing, not the arithmetic; the ledger discipline transfers to it unchanged.

What this chapter does and does not establish

State the boundary without varnish. This chapter and its lab are a declared teaching model of a cluster. The simulators are deterministic Pyodide; the manifests are schema-shaped strings; no cluster was created, no GPU was scheduled, no pod booted. The numbers are course-inherited and named: 165 s from the T09 fleet run (a real measured boot, on a MacBook, of a small model — quoted, not re-measured); the ledger from T10 (declared arithmetic of a teaching 8B); the probe budget, the floor and the revision arithmetic (declared policies). Nothing here is a benchmark of Kubernetes, and a student who quotes "200 s" as what a real cluster waited has failed the reading.

What stays with production, cited but not reproduced: real cluster behaviour (scheduling delays, eviction storms, multi-tenant pressure), real GPU Operator installs, real MIG partitioning, and the OpenAI and llm-d talks quoted as sources for failure taxonomies and probe designs. What stays with later topics: autoscaling the fleet's size — Topic 12 takes the declared fleet and asks who decides how many pods — and the dashboards that watch it — Topic 13, already published, reads the same signals with SLOs attached.

What the chapter does establish is a vocabulary and an arithmetic that run in any browser: declare the pod from the ledger, budget the boot with named headroom, hold the rollout floor and read it off the flags, treat a release as append-only history, and write the runbook that quotes all of it in revision numbers. That bundle — not any specific YAML — is the transferable asset; a real cluster will change the numbers, and nothing else.

Check understanding and transfer

Answer before opening the explanations. Questions 2, 5 and 7 change one condition each: reproducing the lab is not enough unless you can predict what changes when the condition moves.

  1. Size the pod, keep it Guaranteed. From the course ledger — weights 16 GiB, KV cache 4 GiB, fixed overhead 1.5 GiB — compute the pod memory for TP degrees 1, 2 and 4. Which degrees fit a 15 GiB node budget, and what must be true of requests and limits for the pod to be Guaranteed?
  2. Budget the boot, name the headroom. Two probe plans for a pod with a measured 165 s cold start: (a) period 10 s, failureThreshold 16; (b) period 5 s, failureThreshold 40. Compute each budget, decide each against the boot, and name what the passing plan buys beyond bare coverage.
  3. Which probe saves the pod?. With period 5 s and failureThreshold 40, a booting pod's readiness endpoint starts answering at t = 160 s. Which probe number saves it, how many failures have accumulated by then, and does the pod survive?
  4. Read the floor off the plan. For each plan, compute the capacity floor for replicas = 4 and say what maxSurge changes: (a) maxUnavailable 1, maxSurge 1; (b) maxUnavailable 0, maxSurge 1; (c) maxUnavailable 2, maxSurge 0. Which plans keep at least 3 serving pods during the update?
  5. Diagnose the stuck rollout. A rolling update is stuck: the application still serves (old pods healthy), every new pod sits in boot forever, and the deployment reports one unavailable replica for an hour. Name the failure mode, the most likely misconfiguration given the 165 s boot, and the fix.
  6. Trace the revision history. A release history is [1, 2, 3]. The operator runs rollback to revision 2, then rollback to revision 1. Write the history after each step, the content of each new revision, and state the property that makes audits possible.
  7. The sharing word does not override the ledger. One 24 GiB GPU is configured with time-slicing replicas: 4. A pod from this chapter's ledger — 11.5 GiB at p = 2 — is scheduled onto each slice. What does the ledger say, and what does this teach about time-slicing versus MIG?
  8. Fill the runbook, state the boundary. Write the rollback runbook row for the bad revision 3 of the ReleaseSim drill: the manifest numbers it should carry, the command, the revision you expect after it — and the evidence boundary a reader must know.
Reveal eight answers and their limits
  1. Size the pod, keep it Guaranteed. The ledger divides only the sharded part: (16+4)/p + 1.5 GiB. p = 1 → 21.5 GiB (does not fit — the service never starts); p = 2 → 11.5 GiB (fits, 3.5 GiB of headroom for KV growth); p = 4 → 6.5 GiB. Degrees 2 and 4 fit; the fixed 1.5 GiB does not shard, which is why the ledger is not "everything divided by p". Guaranteed means requests == limits on every container — here memory 11.5Gi and nvidia.com/gpu: 2 at p = 2. Requests below limits would make the pod Burstable: schedulable on a promise the weights cannot keep.
  2. Budget the boot, name the headroom. (a) 10 × 16 = 160 s < 165 s — the probe gives up five seconds before the weights land: the pod is killed mid-boot and restarts into the same trap, a crash-loop at cluster scale. (b) 5 × 40 = 200 s ≥ 165 s — passes, with 35 s of headroom. The headroom is not slack: the 165 s is a median from the T09 fleet run, and a slower disk on some future node moves the boot, not the budget. The lab's hidden tests also pin the inclusive comparison — exactly 165 s against a 165 s budget passes — and the off-by-one on when the threshold starts counting.
  3. Which probe saves the pod?. Probes fire at t = 5, 10, 15, … The endpoint answers at t = 160 s — probe number 32. Before it, 31 probes failed: 31 ≤ 40, so the count never reaches the threshold and the startup probe never gives up. Probe 32 succeeds, the pod becomes Ready, and the failure counter resets. The pod survives with a margin of 9 more tolerated failures (40 − 31) — another way to see the 35 s of headroom the budget buys.
  4. Read the floor off the plan. The floor is replicas − maxUnavailable: (a) 4 − 1 = 3, (b) 4 − 0 = 4, (c) 4 − 2 = 2. Plans (a) and (b) keep at least 3 serving pods; (c) dips to 2 — and for an LLM pod the dip lasts the whole boot, 165 s, because a deleted pod's capacity returns only when its replacement has loaded the weights. maxSurge changes none of these numbers: it adds a pod above replicas so the new one can become Ready before an old one dies. Surge is speed; maxUnavailable is the pit. That is quiz q3's "why", and the OpenAI lesson in one line.
  5. Diagnose the stuck rollout. This is the reported OpenAI KubeCon failure class: the rollout hangs because the new pods never become Ready, while the old pods — immortal during the stuck rollout — keep serving. Most likely misconfiguration: the startupProbe budget is smaller than the boot (for example period 5 × threshold 24 = 120 s < 165 s), so every new pod is killed 120 s into a 165 s load and restarts into the same trap. The fix: budget the boot with headroom — 200 s in the lab — and then the rollout completes: the new pod becomes Ready, and only then does the plan allow an old pod to die.
  6. Trace the revision history. Rollback to 2 from [1, 2, 3] appends revision 4 with the content of revision 2: history [1, 2, 3, 4]. Rollback to 1 appends revision 5 with the content of revision 1: history [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. Nothing is ever overwritten or deleted — history is append-only, the same honesty as the T08 journal's hash chain. The property that makes audits possible: every state the fleet ever ran has a unique, permanent revision number, so a postmortem can say "revision 4 was wrong" without ambiguity about what revision 4 means now.
  7. The sharing word does not override the ledger. Four pods × 11.5 GiB = 46 GiB of pod memory aimed at one 24 GiB device. Time-slicing shares compute by rotating time; it does not shrink or isolate memory — the pods will collide in the device's memory and the OOM will hit all of them, not one slice. The ledger must fit the slice regardless of the sharing mode: either smaller pods (higher TP degree, more fixed overhead per pod but less sharded memory), or MIG, whose hardware slices actually partition memory with isolation. The sharing word changes who waits, not what fits — the arithmetic decides what fits.
  8. Fill the runbook, state the boundary. The row: values — replicas 3, memory 11.5Gi (p = 2), startupProbe 5 s × 40 = 200 s ≥ 165 s; command — helm rollback <release> 1; expectation — a new revision 4 with the content of revision 1, history [1, 2, 3, 4]; verification — helm history shows the append, and the running pod's spec matches revision 1's values. The boundary: this chapter is a declared teaching model of a cluster — the simulators are deterministic Pyodide, the numbers are course-inherited (165 s from the T09 fleet run at seminars/runs/2026-11-07-topic09-fleet-mac/README.md, the ledger from T10) — so the runbook is practice for the real one, not a record of a real cluster incident.
