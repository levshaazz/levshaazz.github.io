LLMOps: observability, monitoring & cost control

Green dashboards lie: a burst second hides inside the mean. Build the honest stack on the layer-2 journal — SLI/SLO with named windows, burn-rate alerts that fire for the right reason, a cost ledger with named levers, and batch-invariant evaluation whose verdicts reproduce. The journal hash chain makes the ground truth provable: an edited history is a detected event.

Editorially accepted material, reviewed by independent agents and checked for source agreement and rendering. This is not a human expert review or a completed learner pilot. Notebook execution and learner validation are separate statuses on the topic page.

21:00: everything is green

The incident that opens this chapter is deliberately boring. Every request answered 200. CI was green. The mean-latency chart — the chart everyone had agreed to watch — was flat all evening. And the users were angry, because one burst second had stretched their requests to 7.2 seconds while the fleet politely reported health. Nothing crashed. Nothing errored. The dashboard told the truth about every number it was asked to show, and it was still useless, because it had never been asked about the promise.

That is the gap this chapter closes. "The server responded" and "the service kept its promise" are different statements, and production incidents live exactly in the distance between them. Topic 08 built a gateway whose journal records every outcome; Topic 09 made the unit immutable; Topics 11–12 gave the fleet declared state and a controller. All of that produced facts. This chapter is about turning facts into obligations: a promise you declare before the incident, a budget you can spend, a page that fires for the right reason, a bill with named levers, and a verdict you can reproduce. The instrument underneath all five is one you already own and have already verified with your own code: the hash-chained journal.

The mean hides the tail

The mean is the most popular statistic in operations and the least qualified for latency. Its failure is not subtle: it is arithmetically insensitive to the tail. Take the declared trace of this chapter — 100 requests, 96 of them at 0.8 seconds and 4 (the burst second) at 7.2 seconds. The mean is 1.06 seconds: a number a manager would call acceptable. The median is 0.8 seconds: the typical user had a fine evening. The p95 is 7.2 seconds: one user in twenty met the burst with their whole request hanging in it. Three verdicts from one trace, and only the percentile-and-window pair is falsifiable.

Read the figure as the chapter's thesis in one window: the same trace yields a calm mean, a boring median and an alarming tail, and the choice of statistic is the choice of what you agree to see. Percentile scope is part of the number — "p95 TTFT over a 5-minute window" is a measurement, while "our latency is about a second" is a mood. This is why the course's rule exists: every latency statement names its percentile and its window, or it is not quoted as a number at all. Exercise 3 makes you compute both flavors of p95 on a small sample, so the nearest-rank discipline is yours before any dashboard needs it.

Freeze the vocabulary before the incident

Three terms carry the chapter, and they are worth freezing now, calmly, because during an incident nobody defines anything. The SLI — service level indicator — is the measured number: the thing the journal actually records, with its percentile and window as part of the number. The SLO — service level objective — is the promise you declare over that indicator: this course's working example is p95 TTFT ≤ 2 s per 5-minute window, declared before any incident, not negotiated during one. Theerror budget is what the promise leaves on the table: 1 − SLO. At SLO 99 % the budget is 1 % of minutes — for a 30-day month that is 432 minutes of broken promise the service is allowed to spend.

The budget is the moral center of the triple, because it legalizes failure. A service that never spends its budget is over-provisioned — burning GPU-hours (real money, priced later in this chapter) for reliability nobody asked to buy. A service that spends the budget deliberately can accept a burst second, a deploy-day degradation, an experiment — inside the envelope the promise allows. The exam voice for this slide is posttest p1: an SLI is measured, an SLO is promised, and the direction matters — the promise borrows its meaning from the measured indicator, never the other way around.

Four golden signals, one journal

The Google SRE book's golden signals — latency, traffic, errors, saturation — sound like a monitoring-stack shopping list until you notice that this course's stack already records all four in one place. The layer-2 journal is per-request: every entry carries the timing, the sequence, the kind and the payload. Latency is the p95 of experiment entries over a window. Traffic is the sequence progression per minute. Errors are the 429s of burst entries. Saturation is the queue depth and the waits the admission envelope recorded.

The discipline that makes the table honest is the source field: each signal must name the journal field that feeds it. If a signal cannot name its source, it is a vibe, not an SLI — and vibes do not survive a postmortem. This is the contract the later dashboard is held to: no tile without a source field, no number without a window.

Logs you can prove

Logs, metrics and traces are usually three tools and an integration bill. At course scale one artifact plays all three roles: the journal. Per-request records are the logs; aggregates over windows are the metrics; the request lifecycle from admission to final token is the trace. The reason this course can lean on one artifact so hard is a property lab 09 already proved with your own code: the journal is append-only and hash-sealed. Each entry hashes its predecessor; an edited line fails verification with a named broken_at index.

The consequences are cultural before they are technical. A dashboard fed by a provable journal is ground truth: when it says the promise broke, the promise broke, and the argument moves to mitigation. A dashboard fed by editable logs is a rumor network: the most the query can say is "some process, at some point, claimed". Story 2 at the end of the chapter is the honest horror version — someone edits a log line to hide a breach — and the reason it ends badly for the editor is exactly this chain.

Anatomy of an entry

The journal entry is small enough to hold in your head, and every field earns its place by what breaks without it. seq orders events; a gap is a lost event, and metrics computed over silent holes are fiction. ts windows them; without it "when" is a guess and no SLO window exists at all. kind separates experiments from bursts; without it the 429 rate pollutes the latency series and every percentile becomes a mixture of two populations. payload carries the values themselves — tokens, wall time, tok/s, waits. And prev-hash seals the history; without it any rewrite passes silently.

The exam voice for this section is the failure column, not the field list: a missing field is not "less convenience", it is a specific way to lie. When you build the dashboard tiles in the deliverable, the walk from real entry to tile — entry #15 of the November drill, with its 24 arrivals and its 12 rejections — is the rehearsal that makes the anatomy yours.

From incident to verified fact

Put the pieces together in the order the on-call actually needs. Define — the SLO existed since before the incident: p95 TTFT ≤ 2 s per 5-minute window. Query— aggregate the 21:00–21:05 window from journal entries, not from anyone's memory: p95 = 7.2 s, 3.6× over the promise. Verify — chain check over the window: ok, every one of the 15 entries seals its predecessor, so the number is a fact. Declare — the burn rate against the 1 % budget is 8.3×; page the on-call and state the breach in those terms.Mitigate — tighten the admission envelope and scale on the backlog signal, both levers the fleet already owns; the postmortem cites entries, not recollections.

Notice what the walk does not contain: nobody "looked at the graphs" first, nobody argued from memory, and the declaration quantifies itself. Each step names its artifact — that habit is what the milestone deliverable asks you to keep.

Burn rate: when a breach becomes a page

The burn rate is the one formula that turns a promise into a page. It is a speed: how fast the error budget is being spent, relative to the pace that would exactly exhaust the budget when the period ends. Formally, for a window of T minutes containing t_bad breaching minutes at budget 1 − SLO:

burn = (t_bad / T) / (1 − SLO).

At burn 1× the budget lasts exactly as long as the period — a month's allowance lasts the month. The lecture's worked example: SLO 99 % → budget 1 %; a 360-minute window with 30 bad minutes gives (30/360)/0.01 = 8.33×. An evening at that pace spends a work-week's allowance. The page line at 8.3× is chosen so that such an evening wakes a human now, while a quiet 2× burn — twice the allowance, still ten hours from trouble — waits for the morning ticket. Exercise 2 makes you recompute it twice, once correctly and once with the percent trap, because the trap is the most common way this formula is broken in review.

Fast and severe pages; slow and mild tickets

One threshold is never enough, and the failure modes of "one threshold" are the reason the SRE Workbook pairs a fast window with a slow one. A single fast line drowns the on-call in pages: every noisy lunch trips it, and by the third swipe the real page is swiped too — alert fatigue, which is an availability problem of the channel. A single slow line goes quiet in the other direction: a slow 4× bleed never looks alarming until the budget is spent, and the discovery arrives as a bill. The pairing resolves both: the fast window (10 minutes, burn ≥ 8.3×) pages because severe-and-now deserves a human; the slow window (60 minutes, burn ≥ 3×) opens a ticket because erosion deserves a reader. Edges are inclusive — the T12 thermostat lesson, again: a metric parked exactly on the line must not toggle the policy.

The pairing also explains the deduplication rule the lab enforces: after a page, the slow lane is suppressed for an hour. Without it, one breach pages once and tickets five times — the channel teaches the on-call that pages come in rows, which is exactly the training that kills the sixth, honest page.

Alert fatigue versus silent failure

The two ways an alerting system fails are opposite, and both destroy trust. Alert fatigue is the volume failure: pages that wake nobody's action train the on-call to swipe them, and the channel dies not with a bang but with a habit. An alert must be actionable — it names the SLO, the window and the lever; if the honest response is "look at it later", the honest alert is a ticket. Silent failure is the volume failure's mirror: the mean-latency alert that stayed green through the whole 21:00 breach was not merely useless, it was an alibi — "we had alerting" turns out to mean "we had a page that never fired".

A channel that only cries once a quarter is fine — provided the quarter's cry is real. The burn-rate pairing is the resolution because it prices both failures: the fast lane is expensive to wake but only fires when the promise is breaking now; the slow lane is cheap to read and catches the bleeds. Ask yourself which of the two failures your favorite service suffers from; both are common, and neither is an accident — both are policy choices someone did not make explicitly.

Rules: symptoms, not causes

The declared alert table of this chapter has four rows, and the last one is the design statement. Row one: burn ≥ 8.3× on p95 TTFT over the 10-minute window — page, because the promise is breaking now. Row two: burn ≥ 3× over the hour — ticket, because erosion is readable in the morning. Row three: 429 rate above 30 per minute sustained for five minutes — ticket plus a capacity review, because admission doing its job honestly is fine but a changed load shape is news. Row four: mean-latency threshold — removed. It is the anti-example: green through the incident, alibi in the postmortem, gone by design.

Symptom-based alerts (the user-visible promise breaking) versus cause-based noise (CPU, restarts — those self-heal, or get their own dashboards): the rule of thumb survives contact with production because symptoms are what the promise is made of. A cause can be interesting and still irrelevant to the customer; a symptom is by definition the customer's experience.

The bill: cost per 1M output tokens

Observability without money is half a discipline. The bill's unit is dollars per millionoutput tokens — output, because that is what the API bills per token on completion; prompt-cache hits save prefill work, not generation. The formula is arithmetic: price per GPU-hour P divided by sustained throughput r, scaled to the billing unit — P · 10⁶ / (r · 3600). The lecture's worked example: P = $2.00/h, r = 250 tok/s (the T6 pod model) → 2.00 · 10⁶ / (250 · 3600) = $2.22 per 1M output tokens.

Two habits make the number honest. First, r is measured, not quoted: the 16 November layer-2 run measured 12.3 tok/s on a laptop engine, and quoting the pod model's 250 there would be a 20× fiction; the lab ledger substitutes your own measured rate, and the deliverable's cost ledger is judged on that substitution. Second, the denominator issustained throughput — the number the engine holds under the day's load shape, not the burst spike a dashboard once caught.

The lever catalog: name the lever, move the bar

Cost work is lever work, and every lever is a named mechanism with a first risk. Batching raises sustained tok/s: the waterfall moves $2.22 → $1.39 (−37 %) and the risk is the latency of the batch — you bought speed with somebody's tail. The price tier (spot or cheaper GPU-hours) halves the base to $1.11 and the risk is availability: spot preemption is a capacity promise with a caveat. Admission — the honest 429 — keeps the SLA for the served and the risk is fairness: who is rejected, and is that policy auditable. Prompt cache skips prefill on reuse and the risk is staleness — a stale cache is a wrong-answer machine; note what it does not do: it never makes generation cheaper, and conflating those is the catalog's most common misread.

The prediction discipline applies to money exactly as it applied to the knee in Topic 06: state the predicted delta before measuring, then let the lab ledger reconcile. Exercise 6 walks one full lever decision — both levers together, each alone — and asks for the order you would recommend, because the first-risk column is a decision criterion, not decoration.

429 is a cost instrument

The lab 08 envelope returns wearing a finance hat. A rejection costs nothing and tells the client the truth; a late answer costs the SLA and invites retries, which amplify the load that caused them. Serving the whole burst "to be nice" spends the error budget of everyone — the tail stretch taxes every concurrent user — while rejecting the overflow honestly spends only the rejected slice. The measured drill made this concrete: 24 arrivals at 4 slots plus 4 queue seats → 8 admitted, 16 × 429 with waits the journal recorded (0.74–0.93 s); the burst second the mean chart never noticed is exactly the slice admission sheds.

Fairness is a policy, not an accident. FIFO is honest to arrival order and brutal to bursty clients; the journal records every rejection, so the policy is auditable — and the audit is part of the runbook. When the cost lever says "reject more", the fairness review is what keeps the lever from becoming a filter on unpopular customers. The journal is what makes that review possible at all.

The score drifts

Quality claims are measurements, and measurements need controlled conditions. Run the same 100 prompts twice — batched versus isolated, warm cache versus cold — and six verdicts flip: 94/100 against 88/100, six prompts that pass in one condition and fail in the other. Batch composition changes numerics and timing; the scheduler reorders work; a warm cache hides a regression behind yesterday's answer. None of the six flips is the model "getting better or worse" — the conditions changed, not the competence.

The invariant protocol is short enough to memorize: fixed prompt order, seeded decoding, cache-busting between samples, per-sample isolation. A verdict you cannot reproduce is not a verdict — it is weather. The judge has been grading you under exactly this protocol since Round 01; the next section just makes that explicit.

Judge-grade reproducibility

The table that closes the evaluation block maps each requirement to the failure it kills and the place you already met it. Pinned environment (lockfile) kills "worked on my machine" — you met it in the Round 01 setup and in the Topic 09 image digest. Declared seeds kill lucky sampling — every lab journal carries one. A batch-invariant suite kills verdict weather — this topic's protocol. Snapshot at the deadline kills moving targets — round tags plus the judge's capture. Private thresholds kill overfitting the grader — that is the judge itself. None of it is new bureaucracy; it is your own world with the names filled in.

The drift drill on slide 18 is the worked instance: run A (94) and run B (88), six flips, and the decision rule — B is the score because it is the invariant run; A's surplus is a measurement artifact and gets investigated, not banked. The runbook skeleton

The deliverable's capstone artifact is a runbook skeleton with four columns, and the discipline is that every row names its journal window. Symptom → query → lever → rollback: "p95 breach, burn 8.3×" → "journal window 21:00–21:05, chain verified" → "tightenqueue_slots" → "widen after ten clean windows". "Bill doubles" → "cost ledger by lever" → "price tier or admission" → "revert tier, keep SLA". "Verdicts flip" → "invariant suite re-run" → "protocol B is the score" → "investigate run A's cache". "Chain verify fails" → "broken_at index" → "freeze dashboards" → "restore from the sealed backup".

Incident-ready means each row was rehearsed, not invented. The milestone asks you to fill the skeleton for your own fleet with rows you actually ran in the lab — that is what separates a runbook from a wish list.

Two failure stories, one moral

Story one: the silent breach. Mean green, p95 at 7.2 seconds for forty minutes. Detection: the burn-rate page (8.3×, the fast lane). Mitigation: tighten the admission envelope, scale on the backlog signal — levers the fleet already operates. The postmortem's first line is a journal window, and every number in it was verified before it was quoted.

Story two: the edited history. Someone "fixes" a log line to hide the breach. Detection: the chain names broken_at. Mitigation: dashboards read the journal only, and the hand-edited history is not a source — it is evidence of a second incident, the cover-up. The moral of the pair is the same sentence from both ends: the facts must be cheaper than the narrative. Verification makes facts cheap; that is the entire investment.

What this model omits

Declared simplifications, in the open: no GPU or kernel traces, no multi-node correlation, token accounting approximate, prices flat. The journal is a gateway-level view — it sees requests and tokens, not SM occupancy. A production cluster adds federation, cardinality explosions, and the observability stack's own bill, which is not zero and is itself a cost lever somebody owns.

The reason the model still holds is that the method is layer-independent: SLI → SLO → burn-rate → invariant evaluation does not care how deep the instrumentation goes. Deeper traces multiply the number of fields; they do not change the discipline of naming sources, windows and percentiles. Say what the model omits before someone else discovers it — that is the honesty habit the whole course runs on.

The measurement contract and the deliverable

The contract in one paragraph: every number is journaled, the chain is verified, the percentile and window are named, prices are declared. A number without a source field is a mood; a source without a window is a rumor. The deliverable is your fleet under that contract: one SLO, one alert predicate with its window, one cost ledger with your measured r, and the runbook skeleton filled with rehearsed rows. These are the first four artifacts of the T16 defense story — the capstone week will ask you to defend exactly this, and the supervised agent will be aimed at exactly this surface.

Keeping the journal: retention and cardinality

A provable journal earns a bill of its own, and pretending otherwise is how observability stacks quietly become the second-largest line item. Three honest levers exist, and all three are policy, not accident. Retention: the raw journal is append-only, but thesealed archive — a chain whose head hash is published — can replace live entries after a retention window without breaking verification, because the verifier walks the archive and the head is pinned; the course stack simply keeps everything, and says so.Cardinality: every label you add to an entry multiplies the aggregate space —kind is three values and cheap, a per-client label is a thousand series and not cheap; the source-field discipline from the golden-signals table is also a cardinality budget.Aggregation horizon: percentiles over a minute, an hour and a day answer different questions, and computing all three forever costs three times the store; the SLO window decides which horizon is load-bearing, and that one is computed forever while the others age out.

The exam voice here is the trade the lab's fixture makes visibly: the teaching journal keeps full payloads forever because the journal is the grading surface. A production journal would seal, archive and thin — and the runbook's "restore from sealed backup" row is exactly the day that decision pays for itself. Retention is not an operations footnote; it is the difference between an audit trail and a liability.

Reading a dashboard critically

Most dashboards are inherited, not designed — and the inheritance is worth auditing once, deliberately, with the chapter's checklist. For every tile, ask four questions. What is the source field — and can it name the journal entries that produced it? What is the window — and is it the window the SLO is written over, or a chart-convenient one? What is the percentile — and would the p50 of the same series tell the opposite story? And what action does the tile suggest — because a tile that suggests no action is decoration, and decoration costs attention that a real page will need someday.

Applied to the incident that opened the chapter, the audit finds the mean-latency tile guilty on all four counts: a source nobody could name (it aggregated a proxy log, not the journal), a window nobody chose (the default of the charting tool), no percentile at all, and no action it could suggest — the mean cannot move a lever, because a mean is not a promise. The honest rebuild is small: one row per SLO, one row per alert predicate, one cost ledger, one link to the chain verifier. Four tiles, each with an answer to all four questions, beat forty tiles of inherited green.

The postmortem that cites entries

The postmortem is where the chapter's disciplines meet, and its quality is a diagnostic of the whole observability practice. A recollection postmortem reads like a novel — "we noticed around nine, maybe earlier" — and produces narratives. An entry-citing postmortem reads like a ledger: breach window 21:00–21:40, chain verified at declaration time; p95 by window with the tail visible from 21:04; page fired at 21:06 on the fast lane; mitigation applied 21:11; first clean window 21:52; rollback criterion met 22:02. Every timestamp is a query result, every number names its window, and the disagreement space collapses to almost nothing — what remains is the actual argument: which lever, how hard, next time what.

The habit that makes this possible costs one minute during the incident: write the window down when you query it. The postmortem written from memory at midnight-plus-four will invent a coherent story, and the chain will be the only witness against it. Culture follows the instrument here: teams that verify quote, and teams that quote resolve faster — not because they are smarter, but because their disagreements are smaller.

The on-call day, end to end

Assemble the chapter as the shift it describes, because the pieces are easier to trust once you have watched them cooperate for twenty-four hours. The day starts quiet: the journal is appending, the verifier runs clean, the burn rate idles near 0.3×, and the correct action is none — the dead band is doing its job by doing nothing. Around ten the lunch arrives: the rolling hour accumulates erosion, the slow lane crosses 3×, and a ticket opens itself with a named window and a named lever — readable tomorrow, invisible tonight, exactly as designed. Mid-afternoon contributes one blip: a single bucket hot enough to matter for ten minutes and gone by the next; fast burn 7× — under the page line — and the slow lane never sees it accumulate. The evening is the reason the shift exists: the burst second at 21:00, the fast lane at 83×, the page, the verification that makes the declaration a fact, the admission envelope tightened, the scale decision on the backlog signal, the first clean window, the rollback criterion met by rule rather than by mood.

Now replay the same day with the chapter's instruments removed. No percentile discipline: the mean says "fine" all evening. No chain: the 21:00 numbers are deniable and the denials are unfalsifiable. No pairing: either the lunch pages three times (and the real page drowns), or nothing wakes anyone at all. No cost ledger: the mitigation's price is discovered next month. No invariant protocol: the postmortem's "quality is fine" claim is weather. The day did not get harder because the fleet got worse — it got harder because the facts got expensive. Every instrument in this chapter exists to keep the facts cheap.
