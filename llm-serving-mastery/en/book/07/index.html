vLLM tuning: moving the curve

Four knobs (max-num-seqs, gpu-memory-utilization, max-model-len, chunked prefill), a predeclared bounded search, a Pareto front between speed and tail, and one frozen production profile validated on held-out texts.

From reading the curve to moving it

Topic 06 left you standing on the saturation curve with a defended operating point. This chapter hands you the knobs that move the curve itself. The same model, the same GPU, the same workload — but four named parameters of the serving engine that control which mechanism binds first, how sharply the tail grows, and where the knee lands.

The course milestone says it plainly: "Configuration table + chosen production profile." The engineering virtue is not finding a magic number — it is a bounded, predeclared search: enumerate a small space, fix the measurement protocol before touching the engine, keep every failed configuration as data, freeze one production profile with a forecast, and let a distinct held-out load judge the freeze. When you finish, "tuning" will mean a discipline, not a superstition.

Three habits carry over from Topic 06 and gain new meaning. Errors are data: a configuration that produces timeouts is a row in the table, not a discarded attempt. The tail speaks before the mean: a faster configuration with a worse tail is not an improvement. And a frozen prediction beats a post-hoc story — now applied to the search plan itself, not just the knee.

The Round 03 precedent

Before any knob is introduced, anchor on what the course itself did. In Round 03, the owner raised the speed bar by 2.3 percent with one change: FLASH_ATTN attention backend plus --max-num-seqs 64. This was not the first candidate tried. A Triton variant had been tested first: it was faster on speed, but failed the quality floor (MMLU-Pro score below the gate). The Triton candidate was rejected and stayed rejected.

Two lessons from this single real event, and a third that is easy to miss. First, knobs interact with gates you may not be watching. Second, the discipline of keeping rejected candidates as evidence separates a search from a fishing expedition. The third lesson: the Triton candidate was not a bad idea — it was a reasonable hypothesis that failed a gate the proponent did not know existed. This is normal and will happen to you. The professional response is not to stop having ideas; it is to know which gates exist and to check them before shipping. The gates in this course are documented in the thresholds file and in the judge contract; in your production system, they live in the SLO, the quality suite, and the canary metrics. Knowing where the gates are is table stakes; checking them before shipping is the discipline. First, knobs interact with gates you may not be watching: a speed knob can silently break a quality floor that lives elsewhere in the judge contract. Second, the discipline of keeping rejected candidates as evidence — rather than quietly moving on — is what separates a search from a fishing expedition. When your own search table has a configuration that looked promising but failed, that row is your Triton. Write it down; explain why it failed; do not rerun it hoping for a different answer.

The precedent also frames the budget reality. The owner did not try fifty configurations; they tried a handful, learned from each, and froze one. The search space in this chapter is deliberately small — five configurations, not five hundred — because that is what an honest budget allows, and because the skill being taught is the method, not the coverage.

Four knobs, four mechanisms

Each maps to a mechanism explained in Topic 06. The pinned model configuration defines the axes these knobs operate on.

Each knob maps to a mechanism you already explained in Topic 06.

The mapping is the whole point: you are not turning dials blindly, you are adjusting the parameters of equations you have already derived.

--max-num-seqs is the ceiling on the decode batch size. Recall the batching economy from Topic 06: weight traffic per token falls asNNN grows, because more requests share each forward pass. This knob caps NNN. A smaller cap (e.g. 32) means each step serves fewer requests — TPOT ticks are shorter (less contention per step), but admission blocking arrives sooner because the scheduler cannot accept new work until slots free up. A larger cap (e.g. 256) lets the batch grow — amortization is stronger, but each step takes longer because more requests compete for the same compute, and the tail grows.

--gpu-memory-utilization is the fraction of VRAM the engine may use for the KV pool (the remainder stays for activations, workspace, and the operating system). This knob directly scales the Topic 06 KV budget: the arithmetic ceiling of 125 simultaneous full-context requests was computed at a specific utilization. Change the share, and the ceiling moves. More pool means more resident requests before preemption — but less headroom for everything else that lives in VRAM.

--max-model-len is the declared maximum context length. This is a planning parameter, not a hard limit on what the model can process: it tells the scheduler the worst-case prompt it must accommodate. A generous value (e.g. 4096) means any client may arrive with a long prompt and the scheduler reserves for it — possibly wasting capacity that shorter workloads never use. A tight value (e.g. 1024) plans for what actually arrives, but a legitimate long prompt gets rejected, which is a product decision, not a tuning one.

Chunked prefill controls whether a large prompt's prefill is processed in one step or sliced across multiple smaller pieces. Without chunking, a new request's prefill takes the whole compute step — every resident request waits, and the Topic 06 waterfall showed exactly this as a grey band widening. With chunking, the prefill is split: each slice takes a fraction of a step, and decode keeps earning tokens at a slightly slower rate instead of stopping completely. The trade: total prefill latency is slightly longer (more scheduling overhead), but the interference with ongoing work is smoother.

Predict before you measure

The lab's first deliverable is a table: for each knob, the mechanism it controls and the expected effect on the saturation curve. This table is written and frozenbefore any measurement — the same prediction discipline as Topic 06's frozen knee forecast, now applied to the parameter space.

The prediction exercise is not busywork, and the table is not a formality. When the measurement disagrees with your prediction, that gap is the most interesting data you have — it means a mechanism is at work that you did not anticipate, and the follow-up question writes itself. But the exercise has a second, quieter value: it slows you down at exactly the moment when speed is most dangerous. The temptation when tuning is to turn a knob, see a number improve, and immediately turn it further — a gradient-descent loop with a human as the optimizer. The prediction table interrupts this loop by forcing you to articulate why before measuring what. The articulation is where understanding happens; the measurement is where it is tested. A tuning session without predictions is a random walk with confirmation bias. When the measurement disagrees with your prediction, that gap is the most interesting data you have — it means a mechanism is at work that you did not anticipate, and the follow-up question writes itself. In the mock profile, for example, chunked prefill may show lower throughput than predicted: the model's scheduling overhead (slicing, context switching between slices) consumes more than the smoother interference saves. In a real engine, the balance could tip either way depending on kernel efficiency and prompt length distribution. The prediction is falsifiable; the measurement is the test.

Designing the bounded search

Why not try everything? Four knobs, each with three or more reasonable values, four waves to run, and three repeats for honesty: the combinatorics reach hundreds of GPU-hours. The budget is finite — five configurations is what an honest lab session supports, and it is enough.

The method is one-factor-at-a-time (OFAT), followed by one confirmatory pair. Start from the default configuration (one run). Change exactly one knob at a time to its most promising alternative (three more runs). Then combine the two changes that helped most independently (one more run). Total: five. The confirmatory pair matters because knobs can interact: individually helpful changes may cancel or compound when combined, and only the pair reveals this.

Write the reason for each configuration before running it. The sentence is not decoration — it is the falsifiable core of the search. "seqs-32: sacrifice amortization for tail latency" is a hypothesis with named mechanisms and a predicted direction. "Let's see what happens" is not a hypothesis; it is a coin flip with extra steps. The reason sentence serves three audiences simultaneously: you (the engineer who must later explain the choice), your team (reviewers who need to check the logic), and the held-out run (which tests the aggregate of all your reasons). When a configuration fails, the reason sentence tells you which mechanismdisappointed — was the mechanism wrong, or was the magnitude wrong? Without the sentence, a failure is just a number that was bad, and you learn nothing that transfers to the next decision. The search plan is frozen into a JSON file with a timestamp — the same hash-before-run discipline as the Topic 06 prediction — and any configuration not in the plan does not get run. If mid-search you realize you need a different configuration, you version the plan; you do not silently add rows.

Failed configurations stay in the table. A config that OOMs, that produces timeouts at wave 32, or that clears speed but fails a quality gate — each is a row with its numbers and a one-sentence explanation. The Triton precedent operationalized: rejected candidates remain as evidence.

Running the search: the config table

The lab executes the plan on the mock profile (CPU, minutes). Each configuration runs the same four waves — 8, 32, 64, 128 closed-loop clients — with the same frozen corpus, timeout budget, and journal protocol as Topic 06. The output is a table: one row per configuration, with throughput at wave 64, p95 TTFT at wave 64, and error rate at wave 64 as the primary comparison columns.

Why wave 64 as the primary comparison point? Because the judge scores the geometric mean at 32 and 64, and because wave 64 is where the interesting behavior lives for this workload — near the knee but not past it. Wave 128 is retained for completeness (does the config change where collapse begins?), but the decision is made on the 64-client evidence.

The honest reading of the table requires the same outcome-first discipline as Topic 06. A configuration with higher throughput but 3 percent timeouts at wave 64 is not "faster" — it is trading errors for speed, and the SLO has an opinion about that trade. The error column is not a footnote; it is a gate.

The Pareto front: speed against tail

After the sweep, each configuration is a point in a two-dimensional space: throughput (higher is better) and p95 TTFT (lower is better). The Pareto front is the set of configurations where no other is simultaneously faster and gentler.

The front makes the trade visible. One configuration is fastest but has the longest tail; another is gentlest but slowest; the front traces the boundary between them. The SLO then picks the point: among front members that clear the SLO, choose the fastest. This is not "highest throughput" as a blind maximization — it is "highest throughput that still meets the promise." The difference is the difference between an engineer and a gambler.

Students implement pareto_front() — a filter that keeps only the undominated rows. The function is short; the concept is the deliverable. In a professional setting, Pareto thinking changes how you read every benchmark chart. When a vendor shows you a single "best" configuration, you should immediately ask what it cost: what was the tail? What was the error rate? What did it give up to win on the metric being shown? A single-point benchmark is a one-dimensional Pareto front with the other dimensions hidden — and the hidden dimensions are usually where the product lives. In practice, the hidden dimension that bites hardest is the tail: vendors benchmark on mean throughput (a flattering metric under mild load) and bury p95 in an appendix or omit it entirely. The multi-objective frame (speed, tail, error, quality, cost) is not academic sophistication; it is the minimum honesty required to make a tuning decision that a product owner can sign. A useful exercise when reading any benchmark: draw the implicit Pareto front. If the chart shows one point, imagine where the other configurations would sit — and what dimension the vendor chose not to plot. If you cannot draw the front, you do not have enough information to make a decision, and you should ask for it., you should immediately ask what it cost: what was the tail? What was the error rate? Freeze the production profile

Among SLO-eligible front members, pick the fastest. Write a one-sentence forecast of what the held-out pair will show. Freeze. The profile file is written with a timestamp and a hash — the same freeze discipline as Topic 06's prediction and Topic 03's calibration choice. After the freeze, the policy does not change.

The freeze is the deliverable because it is falsifiable. A configuration chosen without a forecast cannot be wrong — it can only be lucky or unlucky, and you learn nothing from either. This is not a philosophical point; it is operational. A lucky configuration that clears the SLO on one run may fail on the next, and without a forecast you have no way to distinguish "the profile is marginal" from "the run was noisy." A forecast converts the held-out from a pass/fail lottery into a measurement of your understanding. If the forecast says "p95 will be about 600ms" and the held-out shows 610ms, you understand the system. If it shows 950ms, you do not — and the gap tells you where to look. The forecast is not required to be correct; it is required to be specific enough to be wrong. A configuration chosen with a written expectation can betested: the held-out either confirms the forecast or it does not, and either way you know something you did not know before.

Three honest verdicts exist. Accept: the frozen profile clears the SLO on held-out texts. Reject: it fails — the memo says the product needs a smaller promise or a bigger machine. Inconclusive: the run cannot distinguish healthy from lucky — the professional answer is another run, not a hopeful paragraph. The lab grades the freeze, not the fortune.

Held-out: the profile meets new texts

The held-out pair must differ from the development pair in texts only — new documents and new tickets from the V-split. Same waves, same SLO, same journal fields, same timeout budget. One run. No policy change.

The discipline is Topic 03's calibration-versus-test split returning at the systems level, and it fails the same way in the wild. The most common failure mode is not malice but convenience: the team validates against a copy of production that is three months stale, or against a synthetic benchmark whose prompt length distribution quietly drifted from reality. The policy ships, the real traffic arrives, and the tail crosses the SLO on day one. The team is surprised because the validation "passed" — but the validation tested a world that no longer exists. The held-out protocol in this lab is deliberately simple (new texts, same everything else) because simplicity is what makes the boundary between development and validation visible. In production, the boundary is harder to see and easier to erode; the habit of naming what changed between dev and validation is your defense. If the held-out also changes the protocol (different wave size, different budget), a failure tells you nothing: it might be the texts, it might be the conditions. You have spent your one honest validation run to learn neither. Teams that "validate" against a run whose load shape quietly differs from development ship policies that were never tested under their real conditions — this chapter exists to make you the person who catches that.

Stretch: the edge risk and the rollback plan

Every chosen profile has a boundary where it stops being safe. The stretch asks you to name yours and test it deliberately. Common edges: the longest prompt in the corpus against the smallest KV pool (does the request get rejected or does the server crash?); the highest concurrent load you expect in production (does the frozen profile still clear the SLO, or does the tail cross the line?); a sudden doubling of load (does the service degrade gracefully or collapse?).

In production, the edge test you ran in the stretch becomes a canary: a small percentage of real traffic routed to the new profile, monitored against the SLO, before the full rollout. The lab's edge test (longest prompt, smallest pool, highest load) maps to production's canary (longest real requests, peak hours, worst-case burst). The scale differs by orders of magnitude; the logic is identical. A profile that has survived the lab's edge test has earned the right to a production canary — not to a full rollout. The rollback plan has three parts: the trigger (what metric crosses what threshold for how long — "p95 > SLO for 5 consecutive minutes" is specific; "it gets slow" is not), the action (revert to default, halve the load, page the on-call — something a human or automation can actually execute), and the evidence needed (which dashboard, which query, which threshold — so the person executing the rollback at 3 AM does not have to figure out what "slow" means). A rollback plan that names all three is an operational artifact. A plan that names none is a wish. The deliverable is not the test result — it is the rollback plan: one paragraph naming the condition under which you would revert to the default configuration, and what evidence would trigger that decision. "If p95 exceeds the SLO for five consecutive minutes in production, revert to default and investigate" is a plan. "We'll monitor it" is not.

The mock model: honest about what it is

The mock tuning model is a declared synthetic queue with the same knobs. It is not a measurement of vLLM — every figure produced by the mock profile says so. The distinction matters because the mock is deliberately simple enough to reason about analytically: the queue delay is a quadratic in effective batch size, the prefill is linear in prompt length, and the decode is linear in output tokens. These choices capture the qualitative shape of contention without pretending to model the actual CUDA kernel scheduling, memory coalescing, or stream parallelism that determines real vLLM performance. When the mock shows that seqs-32 improves p95 at wave 64, the mechanism is transparent: fewer requests competing per step. When real vLLM shows the same, the mechanism is the same but the magnitude differs because the real engine has additional costs the mock does not model. The mock is a teaching tool that makes the mechanism visible; the measurement is the evidence that makes it real. What the mock validates is the pipeline: the search plan, the sweep, the Pareto front, the freeze, the held-out protocol. When the gpu profile runs the same notebook, the pipeline is already debugged and only the numbers change.

Being explicit about what the mock is not is part of the course contract. The mock's queue delay formula (0.08 × effective-batch² ÷ utilization) is a declared approximation of the real engine's behavior under contention. It captures the qualitative shape — more parallel decode work means more contention — without pretending to model kernel scheduling, memory bandwidth, or CUDA stream parallelism. When the mock and the gpu profile disagree, the gpu wins. The mock exists so that the pipeline can be tested in minutes on a free CPU.

Knob interactions: why OFAT is not enough

One-factor-at-a-time (OFAT) is efficient — five configurations instead of dozens — but it has a known blind spot: knob interactions. If max-num-seqs and gpu-memory-utilization interact (they do: a larger KV pool means the batch ceiling is reached differently), OFAT may miss the best combination.

This is why the search plan includes one confirmatory pair: the two individually most promising changes, combined. If the pair is better than either alone, the interaction is positive (compounding). If it is worse, the knobs are fighting each other — you learned something OFAT alone would not have revealed. If it is the same as the better individual, the knobs are independent. Each outcome is information.

The full solution to interactions is a factorial design or response surface methodology — topics in a statistics course, not a serving lab. The honest compromise is OFAT plus one pair, with the caveat stated: "interactions beyond the tested pair are unmeasured." A search that does not name its blind spot is not a search; it is a tour.

Speed knobs and quality gates

The Round 03 Triton precedent deserves its own section because it teaches a lesson that is easy to forget in the excitement of tuning: speed knobs can break quality. The Triton kernel was faster — it won on throughput — but it silently changed the model's numerical behavior enough to fail the MMLU-Pro floor. The speed gate said yes; the quality gate said no.

In this chapter's lab, the quality floors are upstream: the model, the corpus, and the scoring are frozen from Topic 06. Changing a serving knob does not change the model's weights — but in production, some knobs can affect quality indirectly. A tighter max-model-len may truncate long prompts (changing the input the model sees). A very small max-num-seqs may cause preemption and recomputation, which should be numerically identical but is worth verifying for sensitive applications.

The professional habit: when you present a tuning result, state which gates you checked and which you did not — and check the ones that are cheap to check. In this lab, verifying that the model still produces the same labels on a sample of the corpus costs one minute and catches the most obvious regressions. The Triton precedent was caught because the quality gate was part of the round contract; in a lab without that gate, the same candidate would have shipped. Make the quality check part of your tuning loop, not an afterthought. A useful heuristic: for every knob you turn, ask "could this change what the model computes?" If the answer is yes or maybe, add a quality probe to the sweep — even a simple one (same predictions on a fixed sample?) is better than none. you checked and which you did not. "Throughput improved 8 percent; quality gates not re-run in this lab" is honest. Presenting the speed number without mentioning quality implies the quality was verified — and if it was not, the implication is false.

From lab to production

The frozen profile is a lab artifact. Production adds four realities this chapter deliberately does not model, and naming them is half the preparation for Topic 08.Cancellation: a user closes the tab mid-generation, and the server should stop computing — but a cancelled request still holds its KV allocation until the engine notices, and the freed capacity is not instantly available to the next arrival. The frozen profile was measured without cancellations; production with a 5 percent cancel rate has slightly more headroom than measured, but the mechanism is not free. Quotas: per-client rate limits mean the effective concurrency is not the nominal concurrency — a client that is rate-limited sends fewer requests per second than an unconstrained one, changing the offered load in ways the closed-loop waves did not model. Backpressure: upstream systems (an API gateway, a message queue) may slow down or buffer, changing the arrival pattern from the steady closed-loop of the lab to bursty, correlated arrivals that stress different parts of the scheduler. Observation: the metrics pipeline that tells you the profile is still working — and whose own latency and completeness determine whether you learn about a regression in minutes or in hours. cancellation (users close tabs; the service should stop generating), quotas (per-client limits that change the effective concurrency), backpressure (upstream systems that slow down or fail), and observation (the dashboard that tells you the profile is still working). Topic 08 addresses these directly.

What this chapter provides for that transition is the rollback plan from the stretch: the condition under which the profile is abandoned, and the evidence that triggers it. A profile without a rollback plan is a bet without an exit. The plan does not need to be elaborate — "if p95 exceeds SLO for five minutes, revert to default" is sufficient — but it must exist before the profile ships.

The second gift to production is the search method itself. When the workload changes (new users, new prompt lengths, a model upgrade), the same five-step cycle runs again: predict, declare, measure, freeze, validate. The knobs may be different; the discipline is the same. That is the real deliverable of this chapter — not a magic number, but a repeatable process for finding one.

The gpu profile (owner-run) produces the measured evidence. The search space is small by design: five configurations is a method demonstration, not a coverage claim. The quiet thesis: method scales

Step back and notice what has happened across the course. In Topic 03, you froze a calibration choice before opening the held-out set. In Topic 06, you froze a knee prediction before running the waves. In this chapter, you froze a search plan before touching the engine, and a profile before the held-out pair. The pattern is always the same: make a falsifiable commitment, then let data judge it.

This pattern is not a course convention — it is the experimental method, and it scales. It worked when the artifact was a quantization scale factor (a tensor-level decision). It worked when the artifact was an operating point on a saturation curve (a service-level decision). It works now when the artifact is a production profile (a configuration-level decision). The knobs change; the discipline does not. When Topic 08 puts the profile behind a production API, you will freeze again — this time a rollback plan — and validate against real traffic. The method is the only thing that transfers unchanged across all these levels.

The second thing that scales is the multi-objective frame. In Topic 03 it was weight error versus output error versus task quality. In Topic 06 it was throughput versus tail latency versus error rate. Here it is throughput versus p95 versus quality floors. In production it will be all of those plus cost, plus reliability, plus developer velocity. The Pareto front grows dimensions, but the reading skill is the same: no single number describes a system; the front shows the trade; the SLO picks the point. A colleague who shows you one number without naming the front is either hiding something or has not looked.

The knobs you learned here are conceptual — they exist in every serving engine, but the names and trade-offs differ. vLLM is the community default with broad model support. SGLang specializes in prefix caching and structured generation. TensorRT-LLM optimizes for NVIDIA hardware. DeepInfer (DeepSeek, Apache-2.0, October 2026) is the newest open-source entrant: custom MLA attention kernels, FP8 GEMM, an async scheduler that prepares the next batch while the GPU computes the current one, and batch-invariant determinism — same input, same output, regardless of batch composition. It targets SM100 GPUs and is offline-only, but its design choices (async as default, MLA as default) signal where the industry is heading. When you evaluate an engine for your own workload, the search method from this chapter transfers: declare the space, measure, freeze, validate — the knobs are different, the discipline is not. For the student who wants one paragraph from this chapter: a profile is a hypothesis with evidence. Declare the space. Keep the failures. Freeze the choice. Let held-out judge. The profile you build this way survives contact with production not because it is lucky, but because you know exactly what it claims and what it does not. And when the workload changes — as it always does — you know how to run the cycle again: predict, declare, measure, freeze, validate. The profile is disposable; the method is permanent.

Check understanding and transfer

Answer before opening the explanations. Questions 3, 5 and 8 change one condition: reproducing the notebook is not enough unless you can predict what changes next.

  1. Predict the seqs trade. Config A has --max-num-seqs=256, Config B has --max-num-seqs=32, all else identical. Predict which has better throughput at 128 clients and which has better p95 TTFT.
  2. Move the ceiling. At --gpu-memory-utilization 0.9 the KV ceiling is 125 requests (Topic 06 arithmetic). What is the ceiling at 0.85? What else gains?
  3. Chunking changes the waterfall. Without chunked prefill, a new request with a 500-token prompt arrives during wave 64. What happens to every resident request's TPOT for one step? With chunking?
  4. Design a bounded search. You have 4 knobs, each with at least 3 reasonable values, 4 waves to run, and budget for exactly 5 configurations. How do you choose?
  5. Who dominates whom. Config X: 300 tok/s, 600ms p95. Config Y: 350 tok/s, 700ms p95. Config Z: 280 tok/s, 500ms p95. Which are on the Pareto front?
  6. Why freeze before held-out. Your dev table shows config P at 340 tok/s and 550ms p95. You are tempted to also try P with chunked=true "just to see." What is wrong with this?
  7. When no profile qualifies. After your search, no configuration clears the SLO (p95 > 800ms everywhere). What do you do?
  8. Held-out must differ only in texts. Your dev pair used wave 64 with the frozen corpus D-split. Your colleague suggests using wave 128 for held-out "to stress-test." What is wrong?
