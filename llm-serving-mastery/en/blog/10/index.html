Multi-GPU inference: tensor parallelism

Exact weight splits with all-reduce joins, the predictable knee of the step-time curve and the memory ledger.

Topic pageBook section

This chapter follows the approved Topic10 arc. The lab runtime is the DECLARED CPU SIMULATION: the NumPy split mechanics are real (column/row-parallel shards joined by an all-reduce equal the full matmul within floating-point tolerance — max-abs error 8.9e-16 at tp = 2 in the teacher run), and every wall-clock beyond them is declared arithmetic with named knobs (C = 800 ms, B = 120, b = 25 ms). The real multi-GPU vLLM route (tensor_parallel_size = 2) is an owner-run demonstration with retained evidence; no student GPU numbers exist, and every number you quote is either that declared arithmetic or your own journal row.

One GPU ends twice

Topic 09 handed you a contract: one immutable image that runs the same way on every machine with a container runtime. But everything that image served lived on a single GPU, and a single GPU ends twice. The first ending is memory. An 8B model at fp16 is about 16 GB of weights alone, and the KV cache must live in the same memory — against roughly 15 GiB of usable budget on a common accelerator. When the ledger does not fit, the service does not start; there is no graceful version of an out-of-memory start.

The second ending is the latency floor. Even when the model fits, autoregression is a chain: each token depends on the previous one, and one stream on one device cannot buy a shorter token step by batching alone once the engine is memory-bound. Batching multiplies throughput; it does not shorten the chain. A bigger GPU is one answer, but a bigger GPU is not always available — and it is not the only axis. Topic 10 opens the first scaling axis inside one machine: tensor parallelism, the practice of splitting the weight matrices of a layer across several GPUs, running the pieces in lockstep, and paying a synchronisation beat for every join.

The engineering virtues are concrete, and the lab makes you exercise each of them. The split isexact, and you prove it numerically before any economics. The economics arepredicted before they are measured: the knee of the step-time curve is frozen into a plan file before the sweep runs, the same predict-before-measure discipline as the Topic 06 forecast and the Topic 09 build plan. The memory ledger is itemised, so the "which degree fits" decision is a row in a table, not folklore. And the failure modes aredeliberate data: two transcripts, diagnosed cause → evidence → fix, in the same genre as the Topic 09 tag-drift and OOM drills.

The course motif for the topic is a grand piano carried by four. Each bearer carries a part; the piano moves only when all four step together; and every step begins with the same "one-two-lift" beat. More bearers make each part lighter — but no bearer moves alone, and the beat is paid at every step, at every layer. Hold that image: it is nearly literal, and by the end of the chapter you will be able to compute where the extra bearers stop helping.

Four axes, one first choice

Tensor parallelism is not the only way to spread a model over devices, and part of the skill is knowing which axis a given problem asks for. The map has four rows, and the lab asks you to write exactly one sentence per row — the deep mechanics of the other axes belong to later topics.

TP — tensor parallelism — splits the weight matrices of one layer across GPUs. Every device holds a shard of the same layer, every token passes through all of them, and the join is an all-reduce per block. It pays for the split with synchronisation beats, which is why it demands a fast bus. It is the first candidateinside one node when the model does not fit one GPU or when a shorter step is the goal. PP — pipeline parallelism — splits the layers across devices: it tolerates slower links but pays with pipeline bubbles. DP — data parallelism at the serving level — replicates the whole engine: near-zero communication, and the answer when the model fits and throughput, not step time, is the goal.EP — expert parallelism — splits the experts of a mixture-of-experts model, one expert per device.

The map keeps the topic honest in both directions. When someone proposes "just add GPUs", the map asks: add them along which axis, and what does that axis charge? And when TP is proposed, the map names its precondition — a fast interconnect inside one node — so that the failure mode you will meet later in this chapter (a comm-bound regression on a slow bus) is predictable from the configuration, not discovered in production. Placement is a decision with stated costs; the lab freezes yours as paragraphs before the stretch card of the real vLLM flag appears.

Split the weight matrices, join with all-reduce

There are two canonical ways to cut a matrix multiplication across GPUs, and the lab makes you implement both from scratch. Column-parallel cuts the weight matrix byoutput features: each GPU sees all of the input X but computes only its own columns of Y. Attention heads and MLP up-projections are natural column-parallel: the outputs are independent feature groups, so no join is needed inside the block.Row-parallel cuts the weight by input features, slicing the input the same way: each GPU computes a partial product, and an all-reduce — an exact element-wise sum on every participant — joins the partials into the full output. MLP down-projections are the natural row-parallel partner.

The pairing rule follows from the shapes: up-projection column-parallel, down-projection row-parallel, and one all-reduce beat at the seam. A transformer block carries twosuch beats — one after the attention projections, one after the MLP — and that count is a declared constant of the model you will price in the next sections. What must stay whole: LayerNorm, residuals and the softmax over the full vocabulary do not split for free; they are replicated or re-joined at the beats. The figure above is the declared mechanism, not a hardware measurement — its value is that every box in it is something you will build and check in NumPy before you price anything.

Notice what the split does not change: the mathematics. Each shard computes a genuine piece of the same multiplication; the join reconstructs the full result exactly, in exact arithmetic. Whether it reconstructs it exactly in floating point is an empirical question — and that is the next section, because the whole economics of the topic stands on that one property.

The split is exact — proved, not asserted

The lab's first working artifact arrives early, by the same convention as every topic before: a small seeded transformer block — an input X of shape 8 × 24, an up-projection of 24 × 96, a down-projection of 96 × 24 — and a full single-GPU reference pass of it. You write the shard helpers yourself: column_parallel, row_parallel, andallreduce_sum, with shape checks at the seams. Then you assemble the block the Megatron way — column-parallel up, row-parallel down, all-reduce at the seam — and compare the result to the reference element-wise.

The teacher run of 1 November 2026 journaled the proof rows: max-abs error 0.0 at tp = 1 (the identity split), 8.9e-16 at tp = 2 and 6.7e-16 at tp = 4 — every row within the 1e-10 tolerance, frozen into split-proof.json. Those numbers are real NumPy on a CPU: the only part of this lab that is measurement rather than declaration, and deliberately the part that carries the correctness invariant. "Разбитое равно целому" — the split equals the whole — is a property the journal demonstrates, not a slogan.

Why insist on the proof before any economics? Because everything downstream trusts the sum. If the all-reduce were approximate, every layer of every token would drift, and no step-time model would matter — the answers would be wrong at any speed. The exactness proof is also your first diagnostic instrument: it turns silent mechanical failures (a lost join, an empty shard) into loud verdicts, which is exactly the property the drills section will rely on. Mechanics before economics is not a stylistic order; it is the order in which claims become checkable.

Step time: compute falls, the beat stays

With the mechanics proven, the topic allows itself one formula. The declared step-time model has three named knobs, printed on the deck and in the notebook, with no hidden measurements behind them: CCC, the compute of one step on a single GPU (declared 800 ms); BBB, the all-reduce byte share billed at fraction(tp−1)/tp(\mathrm{tp}-1)/\mathrm{tp}(tp−1)/tp (declared 120); and bbb, the price of one synchronisation beat (declared 25 ms).

Without the beat term the curve would fall forever and the answer to "how many GPUs" would always be "more". With it, the total develops a minimum — the knee — and the whole discipline of this chapter is that the knee is predictable before the sweep. The figure shows the three terms against the degree for the declared knobs: compute falling as 800 → 400 → 267 → 200 → 160 → 133 → 114 → 100 ms, the sync terms rising to 280 ms at tp = 8, and the total dipping to 356 ms at p = 5 before climbing again.

A note on honesty, in the same breath as Topic 07's mock and Topic 08's gateway: these are declared milliseconds, teaching-grade arithmetic with named knobs. The structure of the model — compute divided, bytes shared, beats added — is the honest part; the values are chosen so the arithmetic is checkable by hand. Nothing in this chapter is a hardware benchmark, and no number in it transfers to your GPU. What transfers is the reading discipline: name the terms, predict the shape, then measure.

Freeze the prediction before the sweep

The Topic 06 discipline — predict before you measure — makes its third outing here, and the lab enforces it mechanically. Before any sweep runs, you freeze tp-plan.json with two predictions. The first is the knee of the clean model — the step-time model without the beat term and without stragglers.

Written out, the clean model is tclean(tp)=(C−B)/tp+Bt_{\mathrm{clean}}(\mathrm{tp}) = (C-B)/\mathrm{tp} + Btclean​(tp)=(C−B)/tp+B: for C = 800 and B = 120 it is 680/tp + 120, monotonically decreasing — 800, 460, 346.7, 290, 256, 233.3, 217.1, down to 205 ms at tp = 8. A monotone model makes an honest prediction: take more GPUs, the knee sits at the edge of the sweep, p = 8. That prediction is written down before you know what the full model will say, which is exactly what makes the later comparison evidence rather than memory.

The second frozen number comes from the memory ledger, and the lab asks for it in the same plan on purpose: the minimal degree that fits the declared budget — 16/tp + 4/tp + 1.5 ≤ 15, which resolves to p ≥ 20/13.5, that is, degree 2. You will meet the full ledger in a later section; the plan only needs the verdict, and the verdict is frozen before anything runs.

A prediction written after the measurement is a recollection, and recollections agree with results at a suspicious rate. This is the quiet reason the plan file exists as an artifact and not as a paragraph: it is timestamped by the journal, it cannot be edited after the sweep without the edit being visible, and it converts your understanding into something a grader — or you, in three weeks — can check. If the prediction misses, the miss is the most valuable row in the journal: it points at a term you did not model, which is the next section's whole subject.

The sweep and the honest verdict

The full declared model adds the two terms the clean plan omitted. First, the per-beat price: b·(tp−1) milliseconds, two beats per block at every layer. Second, straggler multipliers: each shard's compute is scaled by mim_imi​ drawn as 1 + U(−0.10, 0.15), seeded by (2026, degree), so every run replays the same "hardware" and every journal row is reproducible. The step is the max over shards, not the mean — the beat waits for everyone.

The sweep runs degrees 1 through 8 through the declared engine and journals one row per degree. In the teacher run the totals read: 818.0, 471.2, 412.6, 392.2, 354.6, 375.8, 380.5, 394.0 ms. The observed knee is p = 5 at 354.6 ms. Compare with the frozen plan: the clean model called for "take more" and named p = 8; the full model bends at p = 5. The gap is the lesson, and the lab requires you to name the mechanism in verdict.json: the per-beat price and the straggler exposure — the exact terms the plan did not pay.

The memory ledger: what divides and what does not

The step-time model answers "how fast"; a second question decides "does it even start". At degree tp\mathrm{tp}tp, each GPU holds its share of the weights and of the KV cache — heads and layers are partitioned, so both genuinely divide — plus costs thatdo not divide: activations and framework overhead, declared at 1.5 GB per GPU at every degree. The declared budget of one accelerator is 15 GiB usable, and the budget judges thewhole per-GPU bar, not the weights row alone.

The rows, for degrees 1, 2, 4 and 8: 16 + 4 + 1.5 = 21.5 GB — over the line by 6.5 GB, does not fit, does not start; 8 + 2 + 1.5 = 11.5 GB — fits, with 3.5 GB of headroom for KV growth; 4 + 1 + 1.5 = 6.5 GB; and 2 + 0.5 + 1.5 = 4.0 GB. The minimal fitting degree is 2 — and notice what the rows beyond it buy: KV room, not necessity, while every added degree keeps paying beats the fit decision never asked for.

This is the section where the piano metaphor earns its wage twice. Splitting the weight is carrying the piano by four bearers instead of one — but the sheet music, the metronome and the bearers' own kit do not split, and the hallway has a width. The "does it fit" decision is a row in a table with named terms; when a colleague proposes a deployment, the transferable habit is to ask for that row — which terms divide, which do not, and where the line is — and to distrust any answer that fits by hope.

What breaks, on purpose

Four failures are staged in this topic, and none of them is a Python exception. The first two are mechanical, caught by the exactness proof; the second two areeconomic, read from declared transcripts in the Topic 09 drill genre — cause → evidence → fix, frozen into drills.json.

The lost all-reduce. A row-parallel partial already has the full output shape: batch × out. Return the partials without the sum and nothing crashes; downstream code runs on a tensor that holds one shard's contribution instead of the exact sum of all of them. This is silent corruption — the worst kind — and the element-wise proof is its designed detector: the max-abs error jumps from floating-point dust like 8.9e-16 to the scale of the activations themselves, and within_tol flips to false. The empty second GPU. A mis-sized partition contributes nothing to the join; the shape checks refuse the obvious cases, and any case that slips through shapes still fails the proof — the sum is no longer the full matmul. Both drills teach the same reflex: a join that was never proven is a join you cannot trust, which is why the proof cell precedes every sweep in the notebook.

The comm-bound regression. A recorded sweep shows degrees 1, 2, 4, 8 with totals 812, 489, 402, 451 ms and compute shares 798, 401, 203, 99 ms. The knee sits at tp = 4; past it, quadrupling the compute shards (203 → 99 ms) raises the step (402 → 451 ms), because the synchronisation cost grew by about 153 ms and now dominates. On a narrow bus — PCIe next to NVLink — the same effect arrives earlier; sometimes the knee drags all the way to tp = 1. The fixes follow from the mechanism: lower the degree, shrink the exchanged bytes, or move to a faster interconnect. The straggler. One step on four GPUs with shard times 101, 103, 99, 205 ms: the max/median ratio is 2.01, past the classifier's 1.5 threshold, and the step time is 205 ms — the max, not the mean of 127 ms. Dividing compute bytp\mathrm{tp}tp multiplies exposure to imbalance; balance and identical hardware are requirements, not hopes.

The transcripts are captures, not live runs, and the notebook labels them as such next to the tables they produce — the same recorded-versus-fresh distinction the course has enforced since Topic 03. Transcripts run everywhere, on any CPU, with no hardware; the classification skill they teach is the reading skill, and it transfers to real clusters unchanged, where the same two failure classes arrive wearing thermal throttling and noisy neighbours instead of declared multipliers.

One journal, eight hashes

The lab's deliverable is a single growing journal, and by the end of an attempt it has a canonical shape. tp-plan.json — the frozen knee prediction and fit degree, written before any sweep. split-proof.json — the max-abs error rows for degrees 1, 2 and 4, each judged against the 1e-10 tolerance. sweep.json and sweep.png — one declared-engine row per degree and the observed-versus-clean curve with the knee marked.verdict.json — observed knee against the plan, with the mechanism named in one sentence. ledger.json and ledger.png — the per-GPU memory rows and the budget line. drills.json — the two classified failure transcripts. Andmanifest.json — the sha256 of all eight, closing the attempt.

The order is not decorative: plan → proof → sweep → verdict → ledger → drills → manifest is the chapter's argument, executable. Each artifact quotes the one before it — the verdict reads the plan, the curve overlays the clean model from the plan, the manifest pins the whole chain — so an attempt is auditable end to end by someone who was not there. The teacher run of 1 November 2026 (seminars/runs/2026-11-01-topic10-tp-sim/) executed all 17 code cells with 0 errors in 1.5 seconds of wall time on a plain CPU — every cell runs on free Colab too, because there are no docker guards and no GPU cells to skip.

Naming provenance per row is part of the claim, the same rule as every topic before. The proof rows are real NumPy on CPU; the sweep and ledger rows are declared arithmetic with named knobs; the drill rows are recorded transcripts. A journal that mixes the three without labels is not evidence — it is decoration. Yours labels them, which is why it can be quoted.

One flag, the mechanics decide

In production vLLM, all of this compresses to one line of configuration:tensor_parallel_size = N. The flag starts the machinery — shards the weights, inserts the all-reduces, wires NCCL over the node's interconnect — but the flag does not decide whether the machinery helps. The knee decides. The configuration card the lab asks you to write has four fields, and each field quotes a section you have already done:

flag: tensor_parallel_size — the one TP knob vLLM exposes at top level. value: 2 for the declared 8B example — the minimal fitting degree from the ledger: 16/2 + 4/2 + 1.5 = 11.5 GB ≤ 15 GiB. precondition: a fast interconnect inside one node — two all-reduce beats per block at every layer, and on a narrow bus the beats eat the gain, the comm-bound drill in data. evidence: an owner-run demonstration with a retained record — measured multi-GPU numbers enter course pages only with that provenance, and student materials carry none.

The stretch placement paragraphs widen the card back into the axis map: TP for "does not fit or shorter step, inside one node"; PP for depth across devices, paying pipeline bubbles and tolerating slower links; DP for replicas when the model fits and throughput is the goal; EP for mixture-of-experts. When TP is the wrong axis, the map names it: a model that fits with a wide batch is served better by DP replicas, which pay no beats at all. The owner demonstration on a real two-GPU node is cited as a card — exactly the boundary the Topic 09 production-variant image drew — so the flag and its precondition arrive in your notes together, with the evidence status stated on the same line.

What this chapter does and does not establish

The lab is a declared CPU simulation, and the boundary is worth stating without varnish. The split proof is real NumPy: the errors — 0.0, 8.9e-16, 6.7e-16 against a 1e-10 tolerance — are measured on CPU, deterministic under the frozen seed. Every millisecond beyond the proof is declared arithmetic with named knobs: C = 800 ms, B = 120, b = 25 ms, straggler multipliers 1 + U(−0.10, 0.15) seeded by (2026, degree). Every byte figure is a teaching-grade 8B example: 16 GB of weights, 4 GB of KV, 1.5 GB fixed, 15 GiB of budget. Nothing here is a hardware benchmark; a student who quotes 354.6 ms as a GPU measurement has failed the reading, and the same is true of any number in this chapter taken as hardware.

What needs real hardware stays with the owner: real all-reduce latencies, NCCL behaviour, thermal stragglers, the multi-GPU vLLM demonstration with tensor_parallel_size = 2— an owner record with retained evidence, cited as a card and never quoted as student data. Out of scope, explicitly: deep PP/DP/EP mechanics (the map is one row per axis), Kubernetes and multi-node scheduling (Topic 11), NCCL tuning, cross-node tensor parallelism and training-side TP with its gradient all-reduces.

What the chapter does establish is a loop that runs on any machine with Python: prove the split exact before pricing it, freeze the knee prediction before the sweep, compare shapes and name the mechanism on mismatch, decide the fit from an itemised ledger, and turn four deliberate failures into recorded diagnoses. That loop — not any particular degree, and not any particular GPU — is the transferable asset, and Topic 11 will reuse it one ladder rung higher: today you scaled one unit across the GPUs of one node with its own ledger; tomorrow a scheduler scales units across machines, and the ledger discipline moves with you.

Reveal eight answers and their limits
  1. Price the second GPU. t(2) = 800/2 + 120·1/2 + 25·1 = 400 + 60 + 25 = 485 ms; t(4) = 200 + 90 + 75 = 365 ms. The realized speedups are 800 ÷ 485 ≈ 1.65× and 800 ÷ 365 ≈ 2.19× — well short of the ideal 2× and 4×. The compute term shrinks with the degree while the sync terms hold or grow: that trade is the whole topic.
  2. Predict the knee from the knobs. Collect terms: t(tp) = (C − B)/tp + b·tp + (B − b) = 680/tp + 25·tp + 95. Setting the derivative −680/tp² + 25 to zero gives tp* = √(680/25) = √27.2 ≈ 5.2. Check the integers around it: t(5) = 136 + 125 + 95 = 356 ms and t(6) ≈ 113.3 + 150 + 95 ≈ 358 ms — the minimum lands on p = 5, exactly the knee the figure and the deck declare. The beat price b·(tp−1) is what bends the curve; without it there is no knee.
