Exam: the paper briefing

The exam is worth 50 points: your grade is the average of your post-test scores. Each post-test is 10 short tasks of 5 points, taken after a five-minute talk on one paper of the list.

The 25 papersExample post-test

How it runs

Four steps
01
Before the exam

Read the list

All 25 papers are known in advance. Read them, take notes, collect questions — you may bring your notes to the exam.

02
30 minutes

Preparation

The speaker receives a printout of their paper. Open book: notes, laptop, network, agents — everything is allowed.

03
5 minutes

The talk

The speaker explains the essence of the paper to the group. The talk is not graded: its job is to make the paper clear to the audience.

04
5 minutes

The post-test

Everyone takes it, the audience and the speaker alike, each with their own variant: 10 short tasks of 5 points, graded by key.

How the grade is computed

The same rules for every paper:

50 points
The exam grade is the average of all the post-tests you have taken.
A task is 5 points
A post-test has 10 tasks. A correct answer earns 5 points, a wrong or empty one 0; a task with several answers needs every correct option and no extra one.
Four task types
Single choice, multiple choice, an open question with a short answer, a calculation.
Your own variant
The post-test is taken by the audience and by the speaker; each person gets their own variant.
The waiver stays
The Overdrive exemption is unchanged: at Overdrive ≥ 10 the exam is credited with 40 of 50 points.

What the 5 minutes should carry

The talk is not graded, but the post-test is written from it. For the audience to succeed, five points should sound — about a minute each:

  1. ProblemWhat the world paid before the paper — slow, expensive, memory-hungry — and who paid.
  2. IdeaThe one key decision of the authors, in your own words, without formulas.
  3. EvidenceThe headline number of the paper and the conditions it was measured under: model, hardware, load.
  4. LimitsWhere the idea stops working or gets expensive.
  5. Link to the courseWhich course topic it belongs to and what changes in production.

  1. 01

    The Tail at Scale

    Dean, BarrosoCommunications of the ACM, 2013Full text on the publisher’s pageIn the source map

    What it is about
    Why the slowest fraction of requests dominates user experience in a large online service, and which techniques make a service respond predictably out of unpredictable parts.
    What to take away
    The fan-out arithmetic: the more servers a request touches, the more likely it meets a slow one, and the tail grows. The causes of latency variability and the menu of fixes: hedged and tied requests, micro-partitions, picking the fastest machines from a pool.

    Orca: A Distributed Serving System for Transformer-Based Generative Models

    Yu et al.OSDI, 2022PDFIn the source map

    What it is about
    A serving system that schedules generation at the level of a single model iteration: finished requests leave the batch at once, and new ones join without waiting for the whole batch.
    What to take away
    Why request-level scheduling wastes GPU time on autoregressive workloads; iteration-level scheduling and selective batching; what it buys — vLLM later called this continuous batching.

  3. 03

    DistServe: Disaggregating Prefill and Decoding for Goodput-optimized Large Language Model Serving

    Zhong et al.OSDI · arXiv 2024PDFIn the source map

    What it is about
    Prefill and decode run on different GPUs: the phases stop interfering, each gets its own parallelism plan, and the metric to maximize becomes goodput — the request rate that still meets the TTFT and TPOT limits.
    What to take away
    TTFT and TPOT as two separate latency targets; why colocating prefill and decode forces a trade-off between them; the KV-cache transfer as the price of disaggregation. Reported: up to 7.4× more requests within the latency constraints.

    Roofline: An Insightful Visual Performance Model for Floating-Point Programs and Multicore Architectures

    Williams, Waterman, PattersonUC Berkeley technical report EECS-2008-134, 2008PDFIn the source map

    What it is about
    A visual model that bounds the performance of a program with two ceilings — peak compute and memory bandwidth — so you can see at a glance whether a kernel is compute-bound or memory-bound.

  5. 05

    FlashAttention: Fast and Memory-Efficient Exact Attention with IO-Awareness

    Dao et al.NeurIPS · arXiv 2022PDFIn the source map

    What it is about
    An exact attention algorithm that is aware of the GPU memory hierarchy: tiling and recomputation cut the reads and writes between HBM and on-chip SRAM, so long sequences run faster and the result does not change.
    What to take away
    IO-awareness: attention is limited by HBM traffic, not by FLOPs; tiling and recomputation instead of storing the full attention matrix; “exact” against the approximate methods it beats. Reported: 3× on GPT-2 with 1K context, 2.4× on long-range arena.

    Ainslie et al.EMNLP · arXiv 2023PDFIn the source map

    What it is about
    An attention variant with fewer key/value heads than query heads: the KV cache shrinks, decoding speeds up, and quality stays close to multi-head attention.
    What to take away
    The KV cache is the price of the K/V heads, not of the query heads; multi-query attention as the extreme case and its quality risk; GQA as the middle ground; how an existing checkpoint is uptrained with 5% of the original pre-training compute.

    Agrawal et al.OSDI · arXiv 2024PDFIn the source map

    What it is about

    What to take away

  8. 08

    GPTQ: Accurate Post-Training Quantization for Generative Pre-trained Transformers

    Frantar et al.ICLR · arXiv 2022PDFIn the source map

    What it is about
    One-shot weight quantization to 3–4 bits per weight based on approximate second-order information: a 175-billion-parameter model in about four GPU hours, with negligible accuracy loss.
    What to take away
    Post-training, data-driven, no retraining; how second-order (Hessian) information lets the error of each quantized weight be compensated by the weights not yet quantized; the 3–4 bit regime against 2-bit and ternary; the serving payoff: a 175B model inside a single GPU.

    AWQ: Activation-aware Weight Quantization for LLM Compression and Acceleration

    Lin et al.MLSys, best paper · arXiv 2023PDFIn the source map

    What it is about
    Weight-only quantization that protects the 1% of salient weight channels — found from activation statistics, not from the weights — by scaling them up. No backpropagation or reconstruction, so it generalizes across domains.
    What to take away
    Not all weights matter equally; activations, not weights, reveal the salient channels; per-channel scaling as an equivalent transformation, and why mixed precision is hardware-unfriendly; TinyChat, their own inference engine, as the speed payoff.

Example post-test

Paper 11 · vLLM / PagedAttention

This is what the audience gets after the talk about vLLM / PagedAttention: 10 tasks of the four types, 5 minutes, 50 points at most. Here it is a self-check — answer and press “Check”.

1 One answer Before vLLM, where did KV cache memory go to waste?

The paper names two sinks: fragmentation — memory reserved for the maximum sequence length is not used — and redundant duplication, when the same prompt is stored once per request. Both shrink the batch.

2 One answer What does PagedAttention take from operating systems?

The paper borrows virtual memory and paging: fixed-size blocks and a block table. The cache no longer has to be contiguous, so almost no memory is wasted.

3 One answer Why does wasted KV memory cap throughput?

Throughput of LLM serving grows with batch size. If reserved-but-unused memory takes the place of other requests’ KV caches, the batch stays small — that is the path from memory waste to lost throughput.

4 One answer What does vLLM report against FasterTransformer and Orca?

The headline of the paper: with popular LLMs, vLLM improves throughput by 2–4× at the same level of latency compared with the state-of-the-art systems FasterTransformer and Orca. The gain is larger with longer sequences, bigger models and more complex decoding.

5 Several answers What does PagedAttention give vLLM? Select every correct answer.

The abstract names exactly two things: near-zero waste of KV cache memory and flexible sharing of KV cache within and across requests. 4-bit weights are GPTQ and AWQ, a quantized KV cache is a separate line of work, and many adapters on one model is S-LoRA.

6 Several answers When is vLLM’s throughput gain over earlier systems larger? Select every correct answer.

The paper reports that the improvement is more pronounced with longer sequences, larger models and more complex decoding algorithms.

7 Short answer What is the name of the table that stores where the KV cache blocks of a request are located?

The block table maps the logical blocks of a request to its physical blocks, like a page table in an operating system.

8 Short answer Several sequences share the blocks of one prompt. What is the mechanism called in which a copy of a block appears only when someone has to change it?

Copy-on-write: shared blocks are not copied in advance; the copy appears only at the first write.

9 Calculation A model has 32 layers and 32 KV heads of size 128; each value takes 2 bytes. How many bytes does the KV cache of one sequence of 1000 tokens take? Each live token pays 2 × layers × kv_heads × head_dim × bytes.

Per token: 2 × 32 × 32 × 128 × 2 = 524,288 bytes. Multiply by the number of tokens of the variant.

10 Calculation A system reserves KV cache memory for each request at its maximum length. 40 GB are allocated for the KV cache, and an average request uses a quarter of what it reserved. How many gigabytes of the allocated memory are reserved but unused?

Unused is the share the requests do not occupy: allocated memory × (1 − the used share). With paging the free part is not reserved at all, so other requests take it.
